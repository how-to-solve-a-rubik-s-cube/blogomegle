The Spare Ticket

Nov 20, 2026 · 3 min read · June & Wes · Austin

June had two tickets and one friend who had discovered, at six-fifteen, that her shift was not optional. The venue was a low room in Austin with a ceiling fan that had given up on being round. The band would start at eight. At seven-forty, June was still holding the spare ticket like a small accusation.

Wes was thirty-three and had come to the door to ask whether the show was seated, because his knee objected to standing and he had promised himself he would stop pretending otherwise. The woman at the door said it was standing room and also that she could not help him. June heard the question. She looked at the ticket. She looked at him.

"I am June," she said. "Twenty-nine. This ticket is paid for. My friend is not coming. If you want it, take it. I am not collecting a story about my generosity."

"Wes. Thirty-three. I will pay you for it."

"You will buy the first round of water, which is the only thing they sell that I trust. Then we will be even."

They stood near a pillar so his knee could have a plan. The band was loud in the way of people who had practiced. June knew the bass player from a job years ago, a fact she offered and then did not decorate. Wes repaired instruments for a school district and had opinions about cables that June found, to her surprise, specific enough to be interesting. He did not tell her the band was changing his life. She did not tell him she was brave for coming alone. Both of them had already come.

At the break he bought the waters. The line was long. He came back with the caps still on, which she noticed because some people hand you an opened bottle as if trust were a performance. They talked about a sister in Round Rock, a landlord who believed windows were a luxury, and a song June had wanted to hear that the band then did not play. Wes said that was most evenings. You went for one thing and got a neighboring thing, and the skill was noticing whether the neighboring thing was any good.

It was. They stayed for the second half. After, the sidewalk was warm and the cars were louder than the band had been. June said she was walking to a bus. Wes said he was walking to a car that sometimes started. He asked if Thursday of next week was a real plan or the kind people make in doorways. She said she would be at the coffee counter on East Sixth at seven, and if he was late she would drink her coffee and leave, because she had done enough waiting for one month.

He was early. She was on time. They did not go back to the venue that night. They sat until the counter closed and then walked the long way to her bus, past a mural someone had already tagged. Wes's knee complained on the hill. He said so. June slowed down without making a speech about it. That was the part she kept. Not the spare ticket. The way neither of them turned a small kindness into a debt, and the Thursday that happened because they had put a time on it.

End.
