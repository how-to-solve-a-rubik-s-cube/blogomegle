A Hundred Meetings · Part 18

Ink on the Wrist

Nov 27, 2026 · 3 min read · Ines & Rafael · Lisbon

The ink was on Ines's left wrist because her phone had died at a tram stop and a stranger had offered a number the old way. She was thirty-one. She had been trying to find a gallery that had moved and had not updated its own door. The stranger had said he knew the new street. He had written the gallery's number, and under it, smaller, his own, with the words if they moved again. Then her tram had come and she had not asked his name.

By evening she had the gallery and had lost the context. Two numbers, one wrist, a smudge near the thumb. She called the first and reached a recorded voice about opening hours. She called the second with the weary honesty of a person who would rather be embarrassed than haunted by a blue digit.

Rafael answered on a balcony that looked over a block of laundry. He was thirty-five.

"If this is the woman from the tram," he said, "I am glad the ink survived. If this is someone else, I am still Rafael, and I will be confused in a polite way."

"Ines. The woman from the tram. I am thirty-one. I found the gallery. I do not know what to do with the second number except to admit I have it."

"You could let me buy you a coffee near the new door," he said. "Tomorrow. I will wear the same green coat, so you can decide from a distance whether I improved."

He had not improved. He had stayed exactly himself, which she liked better. The gallery was showing drawings of stairwells. They looked at three of them and then admitted neither of them was in the mood to be an audience. They walked downhill toward the river because Lisbon makes that walk easy and the return walk honest. Rafael told her he repaired bicycles and had written on her wrist because his own phone was, ironically, also dead. Ines told him she translated manuals and spent her days making instructions less cruel.

"The number was not a trick," he said. "I do not usually write on people. You asked a question and the paper in my pocket was a receipt I needed."

"I do not usually call strange wrists," Ines said. "We are both slightly off our policy. Coffee is a good size for an exception."

The exception widened by a measured amount. They met on Saturdays at a bike shop that also sold bad espresso, and Ines began to bring manuals she was stuck on, not so he would solve them, so she could say the stuck sentence aloud. Rafael began to save the green coat for days he wanted to be found. The ink washed off by the second morning. The number was in her phone by then, under his name, with the gallery address beside it so she would remember that a useful thing and a wanted thing had arrived in the same minute and were not the same. She keeps both. He keeps the receipt he did not write on, blank, in a drawer, a small proof that he had chosen skin only because he meant to be reached.

End.
