The Margin Note

Nov 25, 2026 · 3 min read · Agnes & Callum · Edinburgh

The note was in pencil, small enough to be modest. It said: This paragraph is doing too much work, and I love it anyway. Agnes found it on a Tuesday in a shop on the Mile that smelled like wet wool and paper. She was forty-one. The book was a novel about a bridge inspector, which she had not meant to buy and then did, because of the note.

She read the paragraph on the bus. The note was right. The paragraph carried a confession, a weather report, and a moral, and only the confession was needed. She smiled at a stranger's pencil and did not expect to meet the hand.

Callum was forty-four and came back to the shop on Saturday because he had sold the book during a move and regretted the sale the way some people regret a haircut. The bookseller, who enjoyed being a hinge, said a woman had bought it and had left her number in case the previous owner wanted the note back. Agnes had done this without quite admitting why. She had liked the mind in the margin.

They met at a cafe under the shop, which was not neutral ground so much as the only ground available. Callum recognized the book before he recognized the situation. Agnes recognized a man who looked slightly embarrassed to be a plot.

"I am Callum," he said. "Forty-four. I write in margins because my memory is a colander. I am sorry if I ruined the page."

"Agnes. Forty-one. You improved the page. I am a copy editor. I spend my days telling paragraphs to sit down. It was a pleasure to see one loved for misbehaving."

He had sold the book because the box was too heavy and the novel was, he thought, finished with him. He had not been finished with it. Agnes slid it across the table.

"I already read it," she said. "Take it back. Leave me the note, if you can bear to copy it onto a receipt. I want the sentence more than the object."

He copied it, carefully, and then they had nowhere to put their hands. The cafe steamed. Outside, Edinburgh was practicing its horizontal rain. Callum told her he designed wayfinding for hospitals, which meant he cared about whether a frightened person could find a ward. Agnes told him she lived with a cat who disapproved of visitors and a sister who did not. He asked which disapproval he should prepare for. She said the cat's, and only if he wanted to come as far as the door.

He came as far as the door the next week, and then, because the cat left the room instead of declaring war, as far as the chair. They started a habit of trading books with one note each, no more, so the margins would not become a diary. The original novel went back to his shelf. The receipt with the copied sentence went into Agnes's desk. They still argue about paragraphs. It is the happiest argument either of them has on a weekday. The note was never a code. It was a taste. They recognized it, and then they recognized each other, and they kept the pencil sharp on purpose.

End.
