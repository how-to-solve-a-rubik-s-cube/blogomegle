A Hundred Meetings · Part 16

Sunday Crossword

Nov 26, 2026 · 2 min read · Joan & Pete · Madison

The cafe had one table in the window and a policy, unwritten, that the person with the paper got it. Joan arrived first on a wet Sunday with the crossword and a pen she trusted. She was fifty-seven. Pete arrived second, sixty, holding the same paper, and did the polite hover of a man who has miscalculated a pleasure.

"We could share it," Joan said. "I am Joan. I do not shout answers. I also do not let a wrong one sit there out of kindness."

"Pete. I write in pencil. This is not a moral position. It is fear."

They bent over the grid. She was fast on the literature clues and useless on sports. He was the reverse, and also surprisingly good at rivers. An hour went by in the way good hours do, without asking permission. The waiter refilled their cups and stopped asking what else they needed, which they both took as respect.

"Same time next week?" Pete said, and then looked annoyed with himself. "That was abrupt. You may have a life."

"I have a life," Joan said. "It includes this window at nine. I am a retired stage manager. I like a start time."

"I fix pianos," Pete said. "I also like a start time. Nine is a kindness to the day."

They did not miss a Sunday through the winter. Madison froze and thawed and froze again, and the window fogged, and they wiped a circle to watch a dog in a coat. Joan learned Pete had a daughter in Milwaukee who called on the walk over, so he sometimes arrived with half a conversation still on his face. He learned Joan had buried a husband eight years ago and did not want the crossword to become a memorial. He kept it a crossword. When a clue was about marriage he did not look meaningful. He asked if she thought the answer was four letters or five.

In April she invited him to a piano he had tuned for a friend, not as a test, as a pleasure. He played badly and happily. She sat on the bench with a gap between them that closed, later, when she decided it could. He asked, in the plain voice he used for clues, whether she wanted a dinner that was not breakfast. She said she wanted both, and that Sunday was not to be replaced. He agreed as if she had offered a contract, which in a way she had.

They still share the window. The pencil is his. The pen is hers, for the squares she is willing to defend. Some weeks the puzzle is finished. Some weeks a corner stays blank and they leave it, because not everything has to be solved to have been a good morning. The thing they solved, slowly, was the question of whether a stranger at a table can become the person you save the chair for. He can. She can. Nine o'clock is still the start time.

End.
