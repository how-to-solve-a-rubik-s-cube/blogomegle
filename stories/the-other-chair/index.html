The Other Chair

Nov 18, 2026 · 3 min read · Ruth & Paul · Seattle

Ruth was sixty-eight, and she had come to the central library to copy one page from a city directory. She did not need a romance. She needed a table near an outlet, because her phone was at nine percent and her bus was not for an hour.

A librarian touched the back of her chair. The children's hour needed the long table. Would Ruth move?

The room had one empty seat, beside a man who had spread a large book open with both hands, the way people hold a map in wind. He looked about seventy. He wore a wool coat he had not bothered to take off. Ruth asked if the chair was free. He said it was, and moved a pencil two inches, which was the whole of his welcome.

She sat. For ten minutes they did not speak. Then the book slid, and she caught the corner before it reached her lap. The page was a photograph of a bridge pier standing in fog.

"That one was finished the year I started work," he said. "I surveyed the north approach. My name is Paul. I am seventy-one, so you can stop guessing."

Ruth laughed once, quieter than the children's room. "Ruth. Sixty-eight. I file things for a historical society that can no longer pay me, so I file them here."

They did not exchange numbers. They talked about the pier, and about a novel Ruth had in her bag, in which a lighthouse keeper refuses to leave during a storm that never actually arrives. Paul said that was most jobs. You wait for the disaster, and the disaster is that the shift ends and you go home to an empty kitchen. Ruth said her kitchen was not empty. It had a radio she left on so the room would answer when she came in.

The children's hour ended. Chairs scraped. Paul closed the bridge book and returned it to the desk himself, which Ruth noticed because most people leave that walk to someone else. She copied her directory page. She missed nothing she had come for. She also did not know his last name.

Two days later she saw him at the crosswalk outside the library, holding the same coat and a paper bag of apricots. The light was red. He recognized her before she decided whether to pretend she had not looked.

"The lighthouse book," he said. "Did the storm arrive?"

"No," Ruth said. "He stays anyway."

They crossed. Paul asked if Thursday mornings were crowded. Ruth said the long table was free until eleven, when the children came. He asked if she would mind a chair that was already taken on one side. She said she would mind an empty one more.

They met that Thursday, and the Thursday after. No one announced a courtship. Paul brought the apricot bag until apricot season ended, and then he brought pears. Ruth brought the novel she had finished, then another, then a folder of clippings about the bridge he had surveyed, which she had found because she was good at finding things and because she wanted him to see that the pier still had his pencil notes in a box downtown.

On the fifth Thursday he walked her to the bus. The radio at home was still going to be on when she arrived. She told him that. He said he would call at eight, if she wanted a voice in the room that was not the radio. She gave him the number she had not given a stranger in years. He was not a stranger by then. He was the man who had moved a pencil two inches and made room.

They still use the corner chairs. The children's hour still claims the long table. Ruth still copies the occasional page. Paul still returns his books himself. The happy ending is not a wedding in this story. It is a Thursday that kept happening, and a kitchen radio that is no longer the only thing that answers when she opens the door.

End.
