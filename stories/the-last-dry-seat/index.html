A Hundred Meetings · Part 2

The Last Dry Seat

Nov 19, 2026 · 3 min read · Lena & Cal · Chicago

The bus had one dry seat, and it was not a metaphor until later. It was a fact. The roof leaked over the back half. People stood in wet coats and made the small bargains of a Chicago evening: a pole, a dry patch of floor, a view of nothing.

Lena was twenty-six. Her shoes had given up at the second intersection. Cal was forty-one, and he had the dry seat because he had boarded three stops earlier and had not performed the generosity of standing. He looked at her, at the water running off her sleeve, and stood up.

"Sit," he said. "I have a coat. You have a paper bag that is becoming a memory."

The bag held a lampshade. She had told herself it would fit under the seat. It did not fit under anything. She sat. The seat was warm from him, which she noticed and did not mention.

"I am Cal," he said, holding the rail. "Forty-one. I fix elevators. I am only telling you the age so you do not spend the ride guessing and miss your stop."

"Lena. Twenty-six. I sell lighting to people who then carry it home in the rain, which is me, tonight."

They did not exchange philosophies. The bus announced a street she needed and then skipped the announcement, the way buses do when the speaker has opinions. Cal told her the stop was next. He was right. She stood too early and nearly left the lampshade. He caught the loop of the bag with two fingers.

At her stop the rain had turned mean. He did not get off. He had four more stops and a dinner that was already late. He said, "If the shade survives, the shop on Damen with the blue awning sells the washer that goes with that screw. Ask for the small one." She said she would. The doors closed. She walked home holding the bag against her coat like a rude pet.

The shade survived. On Saturday she went to the shop with the blue awning, mostly to see whether he had invented it. He had not. The washer was there. So was Cal, buying a pack of fuses he did not need yet, because elevators fail on Sundays and he liked to be bored and ready.

"You kept it," he said.

"I sat where you told me to sit," Lena said. "It seemed consistent."

They had coffee next door because the hardware store did not allow cups near the bins. He learned she lived above a violin teacher who practiced scales at seven. She learned he had a sister in Evanston and a habit of giving up seats only after he had decided the other person was actually wet, not merely dramatic. She accepted this. It was a standard, and standards were allowed.

They ride the same bus on Tuesdays now, when her shop shift ends and his last call is downtown. Sometimes the seat is dry. Sometimes it is not, and they stand. The lampshade is on her lamp. The washer is in it. The part that lasted is smaller than a rescue. He stood once. She went looking for a screw he had named correctly. They kept choosing the same ride after the rain had nothing left to prove.

End.
