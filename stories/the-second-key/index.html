A Hundred Meetings · Part 10

The Second Key

Nov 23, 2026 · 3 min read · Rosa & Ben · Minneapolis

Rosa had one key and a lock that stuck in the cold, which in Minneapolis was not a quirk. It was the climate. She was thirty-three and had moved alone into a building that smelled like carpet cleaner and someone else's curry, both of them fine. Ben was thirty-nine and ran the lock shop two blocks away, a narrow room with a machine that sounded like a decided animal.

"I need a second key," she said. "Not because anyone is moving in. Because I will lock myself out. I am Rosa. I am saying the age too, since you are about to know where I live. Thirty-three."

"Ben. Thirty-nine. The key does not get your life story. It gets a code. The life story is optional and not required for service."

He cut it while she watched, because she had asked to watch. The machine threw a bright curl of metal. Ben swept it up before it could migrate. Rosa appreciated a person who cleaned the thing he had just made. He put the new key on a plain ring and tapped the old one against it so she could hear they were a pair.

"Give the second one to someone who answers the phone," he said. "Or keep it in your desk. Both are adult choices. The desk will not judge you. A person might."

"I will start with the desk."

She did. January proved her right about the lock and wrong about her memory. She called the shop from the hallway in a coat she had not zipped, and Ben came with the code and a second copy he had not needed to keep and had kept anyway, labeled only with the number. He did not come inside. He stood on the mat, opened the door, handed her the spare, and stepped back.

"You should still have one that is not me," he said. "I am a business. Businesses have hours."

"Come back at six, if you want," Rosa said. "Not as the business. I make a soup that is better than the hallway. If six is too much, no harm. You already did the job."

He came at six with his boots off at the door without being asked. The soup was lentil and had too much lemon, which he ate anyway. The apartment had one chair that did not wobble and one that did. He took the wobbly one. Rosa told him she had left a shared lease in another city because the quiet there had stopped being peace. Ben told her he had learned locks from an uncle and had kept the shop when the uncle's hands gave out. He did not offer to become her emergency contact as a romantic speech. He offered his after-hours number on a card and said, "This is not the key. This is a phone. Use it if the door misbehaves. Use it if you want company that leaves when you say so."

She used it for both, on different nights, and was clear which was which. In March she gave him a key of his own, not because she was locked out, but because she had decided. He keeps it on a separate ring from the shop's. The shop key opens other people's doors and stays in the drawer at closing. Hers stays in his coat. The difference is the point. She still has one in the desk. She likes having a choice that is only hers, and a person who understood that before he accepted the other one.

End.
