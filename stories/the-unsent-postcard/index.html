A Hundred Meetings · Part 11

The Unsent Postcard

Nov 24, 2026 · 3 min read · Tom & Leah · Bath

Tom found the postcard in a book he had not opened since he was twenty-four. He was thirty-six now. The card showed the weir at Bath, the water white and slightly dishonest, the way tourist photographs are. On the back, in his younger handwriting, he had written Leah's old street and one sentence: I should have said I was happy on that bench, and I would like to be happy on it again if you are still the sort of person who sits still.

He had not been brave at twenty-four. He had been polite, which had looked like the same thing and was not. Leah had been twenty-five then, a year older, and had gone to a job in another city before either of them had called the bench a plan. Tom stood in his kitchen and did the adult arithmetic. Twelve years was a long time to deliver a sentence. It was also, possibly, the correct amount of time, if he sent it without expecting the younger people to answer.

He addressed it to the only Leah he could find who still matched the person he remembered, through a work page that listed a studio in Bath. He wrote a new line under the old one. I am thirty-six. This is late. If you would rather not, ignore it. If you would, I am at the weir on Saturday at four.

Leah was thirty-seven. She received the card at the studio, between invoices, and sat down. She remembered the bench and the boy who had talked about a trip he could not afford and then had walked her to a bus without asking for anything, including a future. She did not owe him Saturday. She went anyway, because unanswered things had started to take up more room than answered ones.

He was there. He had aged in the ordinary ways. So had she. They did not hug as a scene. They nodded, which was enough.

"You mailed it," Leah said.

"I found it," Tom said. "I am not going to pretend I was wise at twenty-four. I was afraid of being a person who wanted something."

"I was afraid of being the something," she said. "We can retire both fears. I restore books now. I live a short walk from here. I am not married. I am not a prize in a drawer."

They walked as far as the bench and found it occupied by a teenager with a speaker, so they stood at the rail instead. The water was the same water, doing the same work. Tom told her about a flat with a bad boiler and a job in survey maps. Leah told him she had kept one of his old sketches of the weir, not out of longing, but because the drawing was accurate and she respected accuracy. He asked if he could see it. She said next week, in daylight, at the studio, where there were other people and a door she controlled.

He came. The sketch was good. The studio smelled like paste. They began a habit of Thursdays that was not a reconstruction of the bench. The postcard stayed in a drawer at her place, old sentence and new sentence both visible. What they built was quieter than the card: two adults who could say, without costume, that they had wanted to sit still with each other, and then did.

End.
