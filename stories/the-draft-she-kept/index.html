A Hundred Meetings · Part 19

The Draft She Kept

Nov 28, 2026 · 3 min read · Hannah & Simon · Melbourne

The draft was five years old and still in the folder, which Hannah considered a kind of honesty and also a kind of mess. She was thirty-four. The email said, in the voice of her twenty-nine-year-old self: I left too quickly, and I would like to know if the lemon tree lived. She had not sent it because the lemon tree was not the point, and she had refused to hide inside a plant.

Simon was thirty-eight and still at the same studio in Melbourne, which she knew because the internet is a corridor with no doors. They had been together for two years in their late twenties. She had taken a contract in Adelaide and had ended things in an airport with a competence that had looked like coldness and had been fear. He had let her. The tree had been his.

She deleted the old draft. She wrote a new one, shorter, and sent it before she could file it beside the other.

Simon. I am in Melbourne for a month. I am thirty-four. I do not want to audit the airport. I want to know if you would like a walk. If you would not, delete this and keep whatever peace you have. Hannah.

He answered in an hour. The lemon tree had died in a heat wave. He had planted a fig. He would like the walk. He named a park and a time and added: I am not angry. I was. I used it up. Bring a jacket. The wind has opinions.

They met by a gate that stuck. Simon looked like a man who had slept and worked and become more himself, not a man preserved for her return. Hannah was glad. Preservation would have been a museum, and she had not come to visit a museum.

"I was unkind in the way careful people are unkind," she said. "I made it tidy so I would not have to see your face."

"You were also right that Adelaide was your job," Simon said. "I wanted the tidy version to be the whole story. It was not. The whole story includes that I did not offer to visit. We both chose the short sentence."

They walked until the wind made them laugh. Hannah told him about a housing project she was documenting, the way a building learns its tenants. Simon told her he had started teaching one day a week and was bad at the first ten minutes and good after that. Neither of them suggested they could have the old years back. The fig was a new tree. They agreed to treat themselves with the same courtesy.

The month did not stay a month. Hannah's contract extended, and then she chose a lease of her own, not a key to his. Simon took this as good news. They see each other on Wednesdays and on the days the fig needs a opinion. The draft folder is empty. The sent mail has one message she does not reread, because the walk replaced it. What they kept from the old story is not the airport. It is the fact that both of them could write a truer sentence, and then live beside it without asking it to erase the first one.

End.
