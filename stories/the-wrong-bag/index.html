A Hundred Meetings · Part 7

The Wrong Bag

Nov 22, 2026 · 3 min read · Claire & Hugh · Savannah

The bags were the same green, which Claire later called a design failure and Hugh called a Tuesday. She was forty-four. He was forty-six. They had stood in the same checkout line in Savannah, talking to no one, and had lifted the wrong handles with the confidence of people who have done this a thousand times.

Claire discovered the error at her kitchen table. The yogurt was the right brand. The lemons were not hers. She did not buy lemons. She found a receipt with a last name and a rewards number, and a box of tea she had never heard of. Hugh discovered the error when he unpacked a jar of olives he disliked and a library book with her name on the card.

He called the store. The store called her, because rewards numbers are a kind of address. They met in the parking lot at six because both of them had evenings and neither of them wanted a stranger on the porch.

"Hugh," he said, holding her bag as if it might be hot. "Forty-six. Your olives are judging me."

"Claire. Forty-four. Your lemons are innocent. I am sorry about the book. I was going to read it on a porch I do not sit on enough."

They swapped bags. The transaction could have ended. A cart rolled between them and a child announced that the sky was broken, which meant it had started to rain. Hugh looked at the book.

"Is it any good?" he asked.

"It is about a house that keeps its own weather," Claire said. "I am only a chapter in. I restore furniture. I like plots where a thing can be repaired without becoming a different thing."

"I teach high school history," Hugh said. "I like plots where people admit what they did. We do not always get those."

The rain made the green bags dark. They stood under the store's narrow overhang. Claire learned he had moved south for a job and stayed for a river he walked on Sundays. Hugh learned she had a workshop behind a house that had belonged to an aunt, and that the aunt was alive, in a condo, and still opinionated about varnish. Neither of them performed a tragic backstory. The olives and the lemons were enough weather for one day.

"Bring the book back when you finish," he said. "Not to the parking lot. There is a bench by the river at four on Sundays. I will be the man with the paper. If you hate the ending, tell me. I can survive a review."

She finished it on Saturday. The ending was kinder than she had expected and slightly dishonest, which she planned to say. On Sunday she found the bench. He was there with the paper and two coffees, one of them still undecided. She took it. She told him the house in the book resolved too neatly, and that her own kitchen table had a wobble she had been avoiding because the repair would take an afternoon she kept giving to other people.

He asked if he could see the table, not as a man offering to fix a woman's furniture, but as a person who had been invited into a sentence. She said next Sunday, after the bench. They kept both. The bags went back to being ordinary. The lemons became a small cake she did know how to bake. What stayed was a parking lot where two adults returned each other's groceries and then, deliberately, did not return to being strangers.

End.
