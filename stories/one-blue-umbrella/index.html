A Hundred Meetings · Part 8

One Blue Umbrella

Nov 22, 2026 · 3 min read · Tess & Noah · Seattle

The umbrella was blue and slightly too small, which Noah admitted before Tess had to. She was twenty-seven and had missed the bus by the amount of time it takes to go back for a badge. He was thirty and had been waiting under a dentist's awning, which was not a destiny, only a roof.

"You can have half," he said. "I am Noah. If we walk at my usual speed you will get the weather and I will get the guilt. So we walk slowly. I am thirty. Slow is available."

"Tess. Twenty-seven. Slow is also available. I am late for a thing that will survive me."

They walked. The umbrella required them to agree on a pace, which turned out to be a useful introduction. Seattle's rain was the fine kind that does not look serious and then soaks a cuff. Tess worked at a clinic desk. Noah wrote grants for a parks group and was used to being told that a sentence was almost right. He did not try to improve her sentences. She noticed.

At the crosswalk he tilted the umbrella toward her and got wet. He did not mention it. She did.

"You are dripping," Tess said.

"I own another shirt," Noah said. "I do not own a way to make this canopy larger. Physics is having a day."

They reached her corner. The thing that would survive her was a staff meeting in a room with no windows. She thanked him. He said the umbrella's name was not a name, it was an object, and he would be under the dentist's awning tomorrow if the rain held, not because he was building a shrine but because his bus also hated him.

The rain held. She came. This time she had her own jacket and still chose the blue canopy, which they both understood and did not underline. They walked past the clinic on purpose, a longer block, so the meeting could start without her panic. Tess told him she was saving for a class in medical coding. Noah told her he had a brother who thought grants were not a real job, and that he had stopped arguing it at dinner. She said stopping was allowed. He looked grateful in a way that was not heavy.

By the third walk the dentist's awning was a joke they kept. They abandoned it for a cafe with two hooks under the counter and a waiter who started setting out two waters. The umbrella lived in Noah's bag. On dry days it stayed there, and they still walked slowly, because the pace had been the point before the rain was.

Tess passed the coding class in the spring. Noah came to the cheap dinner she allowed herself and did not bring flowers, because she had said flowers made her feel like a lobby. He brought a second umbrella, larger, blue, with room for two people at an ordinary speed. She laughed. They use the small one anyway when they want the old agreement. The meeting still has no windows. The walk still does. They kept the habit of choosing a pace out loud, which turns out to be a durable way to love someone.

End.
