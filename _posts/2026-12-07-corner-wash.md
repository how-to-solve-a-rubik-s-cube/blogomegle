The shirt was blue and not either of theirs. It had been left in a dryer, and the laundromat's rule was a lost-and-found bin that nobody trusted. Aisha took it out, folded it, and set it on the table. Devon, not seeing her do it, picked it up, shook it out, and folded it again, because wrinkles made him unreasonable. She watched the second folding and laughed once.

"We have improved a stranger's life by a millimeter," she said. "I am Aisha. Twenty-three. The blue shirt is now a community project."

"Devon. Twenty-nine. I thought someone had abandoned it mid-fold. I was going to be the hero of a very small story. You may keep half the heroism."

They put the shirt in the bin on top, where it had a chance. Aisha's own wash was a set of scrubs. She was a year into a radiology program and lived on a schedule that made laundry a sport. Devon managed a hardware store and came at odd hours because the store's hours were the other odd hours. The laundromat was the overlap.

They did not exchange numbers over the shirt. That would have been a lot of pressure to put on a stranger's sleeve. They simply both came back on Thursday, and both pretended, badly, that Thursday had always been the plan. Aisha sat on the table and read a chapter. Devon replaced a button on a coat. The machines turned. Conversation started around the edges: a landlord, a sister, a teacher who had said Aisha was precise. Devon said precise was a compliment people gave when they were slightly afraid of you. Aisha said she could live with that.

"I am not afraid of you," he added. "I am afraid of asking you to get a sandwich and making the shirt into a myth. So I will ask badly and plainly. Sandwich. Next door. When the dryers stop."

"Ask badly more often," Aisha said. "Yes."

The sandwich place knew neither of them. They took a window and watched their bags through the glass like parents of inanimate objects. Devon told her he had wanted to be a carpenter and had become a man who sold other people the nails, and that this was not a tragedy, just a turn. Aisha told him she liked the turn. Sellers of nails kept hospitals standing too. He looked as if a weight had shifted, not vanished.

They share Thursday now, and a number in the phone, and a joke about the blue shirt that was never claimed. Aisha's program is harder than the folding. Devon brings food on the weeks she would otherwise forget. She brings a precision he borrows for his own night classes, started in the fall, because the turn was allowed to turn again. The laundromat is still too bright and too warm. They like it. It is where a shirt got folded twice and a question got asked badly, which was the right way.

**End.**
