The dining room was reset and slightly ghostly, chairs up, one table down. Colette sat at it with a plate she had made for herself: beans, rice, the last of a fish the specials board had overpromised. She was thirty-three, the chef, and she did not like to be watched while she ate. Miles knew that. He was thirty and had been washing pans until his hands were a color he did not discuss. He hovered with a bowl of the same beans.

"I can eat in the alley," he said.

"You can eat here," Colette said. "If you talk, talk about something that is not the service. The service is over. I am a person. You are a person. Sit."

He sat. For a few minutes they were only hungry, which was a decent way to begin. The street outside still had music in it, thin at this hour, a trumpet practicing rather than performing. Miles said his brother played and was better than the practice suggested. Colette said she had grown up over a bakery in Metairie and had learned early that butter covered a number of sins and created others.

"I am Miles," he said, late, because the names had been assumed by the kitchen and he wanted this table to have the real ones. "Thirty. I am not going to wash dishes forever. I am also not in a rush to be a story about ambition. I like the work. I like it more when it ends."

"Colette. Thirty-three. I am already what I trained to be. Some nights that feels like a prize. Some nights it feels like a narrow door. Tonight it feels like beans, which is acceptable."

They did the dishes together after, even though his shift was over and hers included a clipboard. She washed. He dried. The arrangement was new and obviously temporary and neither of them made a speech about teamwork. When the last pan was up, Miles walked her to her bike because the block was empty and because he wanted three more minutes. He did not ask to come home. She did not pretend not to notice the not-asking. She told him she did Sundays off, and that if he wanted a meal that was not a leftover, she would cook at noon and he could bring oranges.

He brought oranges and a bunch of flowers he had clearly argued with himself about, inexpensive and still chosen. Colette put them in a jar that had held jam. The apartment was small and faced a wall and was hers. They ate slowly. Miles left at three because he had a shift and because leaving on time was a way of saying he could be trusted with a next time.

There is a next time every Sunday now, and some Mondays after service, at the one table left down. The staff know and do not comment, which is the kindness Colette required. Miles is learning the sauté station on slow nights, not as a reward for sitting with her, as a plan he had already made. She corrects his hands without correcting his dignity. He tells her when the beans need salt. The trumpet down the street still practices. They do not need it to become a concert. The last table is enough of a room for two people who feed other people all night and then, finally, feed each other.

**End.**
