The railing failed the height rule by an inch and a half. Bob had the tape out. He was forty-two, and he did not enjoy being the person who arrived from elsewhere to tell a town its favorite walk was illegal. Carol had the file. She was thirty-nine, the clerk, and she had walked that railing since she was old enough to ignore her mother, which she mentioned only to establish that affection was not the same as compliance.

"If you write it up as a hazard," she said, "the council will panic and close the span, and then people will cross the highway instead, which is a better way to die. I am Carol. I would like a repair, not a closure. I am saying so before you finish the number."

"Bob. I was already going to recommend a repair. The inch and a half is real. So is the highway. I am not a villain in a truck. I am a man with a tape."

The lake was a hard blue and the wind took the paperwork. Carol caught it. Their hands met on the folder and left. They walked the span so Bob could photograph the posts, and Carol named, without nostalgia, the places the ice heaved in April. She knew the bridge the way a person knows a tool. He respected that more than he respected agreement.

At the far end there was a cafe that served pie in slices large enough to be a decision. Bob had an hour before his next town. Carol had a meeting she could start late if she fed it an excuse. They used the hour.

"I live here," Carol said. "I am not a stop on a route. If you write a decent report, I will see that it is read. If you want dinner the next time you are in Duluth, say so as yourself. Do not hide it in a work order."

"Dinner," Bob said. "As myself. I am in town on the nineteenth. Forty-two. I like a clerk who catches paper before it becomes litter."

"Thirty-nine. I like an inspector who can hold two risks in his head at once."

The report recommended a welded extension and a temporary sign, not a closure. The council grumbled and then voted. On the nineteenth Bob came back without the tape, in a better jacket, and Carol met him at the cafe in a coat that was not her office coat. They ate pie again because the first pie had been accurate. After, they walked the bridge. The sign was up. The repair was scheduled. Carol put her hand on the old rail and then took it off, a habit, and Bob did not grab it to make a point. He walked on the traffic side, between her and the road, without announcing the choice.

The extension is on the railing now. It is uglier and taller and it keeps people. Bob's route still brings him through Duluth, and his evenings there are no longer in the motel. Carol keeps the file in a drawer, closed, because the bridge is not their metaphor. It is a bridge. They are two adults who argued about an inch and a half, agreed on the part that mattered, and then kept walking long after the report was filed.

**End.**
