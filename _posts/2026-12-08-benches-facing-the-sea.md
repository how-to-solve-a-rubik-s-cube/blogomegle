The benches faced the same gray water and were close enough that a conversation could happen without either person moving house. For a month it did not. Ida sat on the left with a book she sometimes read. She was seventy. Arthur sat on the right with a flask and the paper. He was seventy-two. They arrived within ten minutes of each other and left the same way, a courtesy neither had negotiated.

The month broke on a morning when her book blew shut and his paper blew open and both of them lunged and missed, and the pages went toward the pebbles. They gathered what they could. A gull supervised.

"Your sports section is under my shoe," Ida said. "I am Ida. I am going to hand it to you and not comment on the team."

"Arthur. The team deserves the comment. Seventy-two. Your page is the one with the soup on it. I rescued it anyway."

"Seventy. The soup was optimistic."

They sat, still on separate benches, and talked across the gap as if it were a table. Ida had been a pharmacist. Arthur had been a harbor pilot. Both of them had lost spouses, and both of them had done the long work of learning to eat alone without turning it into a personality. The sea made a sound that covered the awkward parts. Arthur offered the flask. Ida had her own. They toasted, ridiculously, with plastic cups, and did not pretend it was more than tea.

The next morning he brought two cups and a question, delivered to the gap.

"Would you like the right-hand bench, or shall I come to the left? I am asking before I arrive, so you can have the day you planned if the day you planned was solitude."

"Come to the left," Ida said. "Bring the flask. Leave the sports section if it is going to make you shout."

He came to the left. The shouting was minimal. They found a bakery that did a bun worth the walk, and the walk became part of the sitting. Ida's children were suspicious in the way of loving adults who forget their parents are adults. She told them Arthur's name and his bench and invited them to mind their own pastries. They came around. Arthur's son came down from London and shook Ida's hand as if she were a dignitary, which made her laugh, and the laugh settled the room.

They still face the sea. Some mornings one of them needs the old gap, and they take separate benches without punishment. Most mornings they share the left one, a newspaper, a book, a flask, and the knowledge that a month of adjacent silence was not wasted time. It was how two people learned the other was safe to sit beside. The water does not care. They do, in the specific, daily way that lasts longer than a declaration.

**End.**
