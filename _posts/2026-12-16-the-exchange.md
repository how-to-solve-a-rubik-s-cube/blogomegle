The notice on the cafe board said: French for English, one hour, no romance, no homework. Chantal had written it. She was thirty-two and tired of apps that congratulated her for buying imaginary apples. Andre answered because his English stalled at the exact moment a customer became complicated. He was thirty-four and ran a small bike repair that did not need poetry. It needed the sentence for a delay.

They met on a Wednesday and obeyed the sign. Fifty minutes of verbs, ten of whatever the week had actually contained. Chantal corrected him without wincing. Andre corrected her without showing off. It was the least flirtatious hour in the neighborhood, which was why it worked. At the end he said, in careful English, "Same time next week is useful." She said, in careful French, "Useful is a good word."

By the fourth week the sign's ban on romance had become a joke they had earned. Andre walked her to the Metro because the street was icy and because the hour had ended mid-sentence, the way good classes do. They finished the sentence on the sidewalk. Chantal talked about a library job and a father who still called her translations a hobby. Andre talked about a sister in Laval and a knee the cold disliked. At the turnstile she did not go through.

"The sign was to keep me safe from strangers," she said. "You are less of a stranger. If you want a walk that is not a lesson on Sunday, I am free. We can still do Wednesday. I am not replacing the useful hour. I am adding a pleasant one."

"Add it," Andre said. "I will bring my slow English and my better coat."

Sunday was a walk along the canal, no notebooks. Wednesday stayed strict, which they both loved; it meant the attraction was not an excuse to stop learning. Andre’s customers received clearer news about delays. Chantal’s father received a description of a man who fixed things and also showed up, and he revised the word hobby only slightly, which she decided was victory enough.

They live above the shop now, in rooms that smell like rubber and coffee. The cafe board has a new notice, in her handwriting, offering the hour to someone else. Their own hour moved to the kitchen table, where the corrections are still kind and the walk is just the hallway if the ice is bad. The sign said no romance. They broke that clause on purpose, out loud, and kept every other rule that made the hour worth having. Useful, and pleasant, in the same week. It is a good bilingual life.

**End.**
