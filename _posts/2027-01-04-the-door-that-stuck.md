The door stuck on a Thursday of actual humidity, not symbolic humidity. Tara could get out and could not, for a while, get the door to agree about coming back in. She was twenty-eight. Kyle lived across the hall and owned a plane for reasons that had seemed fussy until they were useful. He was thirty.

"I can take a millimeter off the top," he said. "It will be ugly for a day and then it will close. Or we can wait for October and you can live in the hall. I am not in charge of your door. I am offering a tool."

"Take the millimeter," Tara said. "I will hold the flashlight. If you start a speech about how we are all swollen doors, I will use the flashlight as a weapon."

"No speech. A door."

He planed the edge, vacuumed the dust, and tested the latch three times. It caught and released. Tara felt a gratitude that was larger than the task and did not, for once, embarrass her. She ordered sandwiches. They ate on the floor because the table was covered in mail she had been avoiding, and she said so, and he did not offer to organize her. He offered a second sandwich. She accepted.

"I have liked you for a semester," Kyle said, because they took a class on the same night and had been doing the parallel walk to the subway. "I did not plane your door as a tactic. I planed it because it stuck. Now that it opens, I am going to ask you to the class walk on purpose, as a date, and you can say no and I will still be the neighbor with the plane."

"Yes," Tara said. "To the walk. The door stays a door. I do not want a relationship that is mostly a hardware problem we feel poetic about. I want the walk, and then a calendar, and then, if we are any good at it, a key of my own giving."

They were good at it. The walk became the calendar. The key was given in the winter, when the door fit loosely and the humor of that was allowed exactly once. Kyle never repeated the swollen-door theory. Tara never needed him to. When something in the apartment sticks now, they fix it and eat. The metaphor stays small. The life does not. Two people in Philadelphia learned the difference, which is a better education than the night class, and they still go to the class anyway, because they like the walk home more than they like being improved.

**End.**
