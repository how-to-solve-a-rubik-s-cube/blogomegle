The market was legal at eight and alive at seven-twenty. Pat had the buckets out and the good scissors in her apron. She was fifty-four and had done this long enough to know which men were going to ask for roses and then flinch at the price. Diane was forty-nine and wanted the small mixed bunch, the one that looked as if it had been picked by a person and not by a strategy. She taught year four. She bought the flowers for the classroom, not for a lover, and she was tired of the assumption.

"They are for a windowsill," Diane said, paying. "Twenty-eight children will destroy them by Friday. I buy them anyway. I am Diane."

"Pat. I sell them anyway. Fifty-four. The early hour is the only time this street tells the truth. You are welcome in it."

They began to talk in the margin before the crowds. Diane learned that Pat grew some of the stock on a railway allotment and bought the rest at a worse hour than this one. Pat learned that Diane lived alone on purpose since a divorce that had been mostly about silence, and that she did not want a diagnosis of her life with the change. The flowers were wrapped in last week's news. Diane liked a seller who did not add a ribbon she would have to remove.

One Thursday the mixed bunch was already gone, sold to a restaurant. Pat had set aside a lesser bunch, not pretty, still honest, and held it under the table for the teacher she was not sure would come. Diane came. The holding had been a risk. Pat handed it over without a speech.

"I saved you the awkward ones," she said. "If that was presumptuous, tell me and I will sell them to a man who wants a metaphor."

"It was accurate," Diane said. "Come to the school gate at four if you want to see where they actually live. I will not make you meet the children. I will buy you a tea after."

Pat came. The windowsill was a riot of stems and paper. She did not cry or clap. She nodded, which Diane felt in her chest as a precise compliment. Tea was at a cafe that still had sugar in packets. Pat said she had a daughter in Leeds who thought flowers were not a pension. Diane said she had a pension and it was not as interesting as a bucket. They laughed the laugh of women who have done the numbers.

Fridays became tea, and then some Sundays became the allotment, where Diane was bad at thinning seedlings and good at company. The market still starts before eight. The children still destroy the awkward bunches, and Pat still saves one when she can. What grew between them is not a bouquet. It is a gate at four, a pair of gloves, a woman who sells and a woman who teaches, both of them done with performances, walking back through a city that gets loud on schedule and leaving each other before it does, or sometimes not leaving at all.

**End.**
