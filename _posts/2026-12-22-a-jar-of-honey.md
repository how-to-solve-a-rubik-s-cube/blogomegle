The jar was meant for a neighbor who had shoveled Doris's step after a storm. Doris left it on the wrong porch because the houses on that stretch of road looked alike once the snow had edited them. She was fifty-eight. The label said her name and the word clover. Robert found it at dawn, read the label, and knew the honey was not for him because he had not shoveled anything but his own path. He was sixty-three.

He walked it back before breakfast, which meant he arrived in a coat and a hunger he had not planned to share. Doris was already sorry. She had realized the mistake when the right neighbor called to thank her for nothing.

"I am Robert, three doors the wrong way," he said. "This is yours to give to someone else. I did not open it. I thought about opening it. I want credit for the thought I did not act on."

"Doris. You have the credit. Come in while I write a better note. The step is icy and I will not have a man fall because I cannot tell houses apart."

He came in. The kitchen smelled like toast. She made a second slice because not making it would have been a performance of busyness. The honey, opened after all, was on both slices. It tasted like the field behind her shed, which she managed with a calm that impressed him more than the jar. Robert kept bees only in the sense that he bought from people who did. He repaired violins in a room that had been a parlor. Doris taught piano. The overlap made them both suspicious and pleased.

"If I ordered a jar on purpose," Robert said, "would you deliver it to the correct door, and would you want coffee when you did?"

"Yes and yes," Doris said. "Fifty-eight. I am not a subscription. I am a woman who made a mistake and a good breakfast. Do not scale me."

"Sixty-three. One jar. One coffee. No scaling."

The coffee became a weekly lid on the week. Doris met the violins and did not touch them. Robert met the piano and turned pages when asked, a skill he had from a life of quartets heard from the workbench. The neighbor who had shoveled eventually got a jar and a note that named the mix-up, and she laughed and told them both to stop apologizing. They did. The road in snow still confuses visitors. It does not confuse them. Robert's porch has a light Doris can see from her turn, and her porch has a bell he can find in the dark. The honey is a household staple and a joke. The toast is often for two. A wrong doorstep was only wrong until someone walked the jar back and was invited to stay for the slice that made the walking worth it.

**End.**
