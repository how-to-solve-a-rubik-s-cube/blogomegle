The first photo made Stefan look as if he had just lost an argument with a bright light. He held the print at arm's length in the shop and considered a life of never traveling again. Mila, on the other side of the counter, had taken it. She was thirty-seven and had a quota of kindness that did not include lying about chins.

"It is the face you made when I said hold still," she said. "Not the face you walked in with. We can do another. Relax your jaw. Think of a minor pleasure, not a vacation. Vacations make people bare their teeth. I am Mila."

"Stefan. Forty-one. A minor pleasure is coffee that is too hot. I will think of that."

The second photo looked like him. He bought two prints and, because the relief made him honest, said, "You were right to refuse the first one. Most shops would have taken the money."

"I like repeat customers more than I like a quick sale," Mila said. "Also I have to look at these faces all day. I am not interested in multiplying grimaces."

He came back a week later without a photographic need. The bell over the door announced him. Mila looked up from a batch of student cards and did not pretend to be confused.

"The coffee that is too hot is two doors down," Stefan said. "I am between jobs this month, which is a sentence I practiced so it would not sound like weather. If you take a break, I will be the man not grimacing."

Her break was twenty minutes. They used all of it. The coffee was too hot. They waited, competent adults, and talked while it cooled. Mila had taken over the shop from an uncle and was slowly replacing the backdrop that looked like a thunderstorm. Stefan had been a project manager for a tram line and was deciding whether to do it again in another city. She asked whether he actually liked the work. He said he liked the moment a line opened and strangers suddenly had a route. He did not like the meetings. She said that was a respectable ratio.

The twenty minutes became a closing-time walk along a canal that smelled like cold iron. Stefan did not ask to see her life in a week. He asked when the thunderstorm backdrop would die. Mila said Saturday. He came on Saturday and held the ladder, which was the correct offer, and did not redesign the wall. The new backdrop was a plain gray. Faces looked like faces against it. She took his picture once more, for no document, and he did not hate it. The print is on a fridge now, in the flat they share above a different street. He still thinks of minor pleasures when a camera comes out. She still tells him the truth. The passport got him onto a train that brought him home. The better picture is the one that never had to prove he was allowed to cross a border. It only had to look like a man who had found a reason to relax his jaw.

**End.**
