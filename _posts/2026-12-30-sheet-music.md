The practice room was booked under a name that had nothing to do with either of them, a cancellation Lukas had taken because his own slot was during a shift he could not move. He was twenty-nine. On the stand was a pencil score, half a song, with a coffee stain and a phone number in the margin that might have been a metronome marking and was not. He played the half. It was good. It stopped in the middle of a phrase that knew where it wanted to go and had not been told it could.

He texted the number. I have your page. It is unfinished and I stopped where you stopped. If you want it, room 4 is free until four. Lukas.

Klara arrived out of breath and prepared to be annoyed. She was thirty-one. The annoyance died when she saw the page uncreased and the bench shared, not stolen.

"I forget things when the melody is louder than the exit," she said. "Thank you for not improving it. I am Klara. Improving it is my job. Other people's improvements are how songs die."

"Then I will sit on my hands," Lukas said. "I play here because my building hates a cello. I am a nurse. The cello is the part of the week that does not beep."

She took the page. He took the corner of the bench she offered. Klara played the phrase and then the next one, which she had in her head and not on the paper. Lukas listened without performing listening. At four the next student knocked and they stood up like people who respected a booking, a quality both of them found attractive out of all proportion.

"There is a cafe under this building that is used to cases," Klara said. "If you want twenty minutes of the rest of the song, spoken, not played, come. If the number was only for the lost page, you have done the decent thing and you can go and be decent somewhere else."

He wanted the twenty minutes. The song, spoken, was about a sister and a train, and it was not confessional in a way that asked him to clap. He told her about the night shift and the cello and a landlord. They exchanged the practical kinds of information. The romance was that neither of them rushed the phrase.

The page got finished. Lukas heard it in a small room a month later, sitting in a row with strangers, and Klara did not dedicate it aloud. She looked at the third row once. That was the dedication. They are past the practice-room booking now. The cello lives in a corner that does not hate it, because the corner is theirs. Klara still writes in pencil. Lukas still does not improve her lines. He makes dinner while she works, and sometimes she asks him to hum a pitch, and he does, and then he goes back to the onions, which is a collaboration they trust.

**End.**
