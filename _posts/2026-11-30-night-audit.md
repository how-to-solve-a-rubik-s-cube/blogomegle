The lobby at two was a lamp, a bell, and Lila, who was twenty-six and did the night audit with a precision that made the morning manager look gifted. Graham came down in a sweater because his room was fine and his head was not. He was forty-three. He had a funeral in the morning. He said so immediately, so she would not think he was wandering for sport.

"I do not need a conversation," he said. "I need to be a person in a room that is not the room where the suit is hanging. I am Graham."

"Lila. The chair by the window is empty. I will be adding numbers. If you want silence, take it. If you want the worst coffee in Charleston, say so."

He took the chair and, after a while, the coffee. It was as advertised. He held it anyway. Lila did not ask who had died. She told him, when the adding paused, that she was from a town up the river and worked nights so she could study in the afternoon for a certificate in accounting. He told her he restored boats and that the person in the morning was his father, and that they had argued about a hull the last time they spoke, and that the argument was small and now enormous.

"Small arguments can stay small," Lila said. "The size changes because of the ending, not because you were cruel. I am not a minister. I am a woman with a ledger. But I have watched people check out after bad nights, and the ones who do all right are the ones who eat breakfast."

"Then I will eat breakfast," Graham said. "If you are off at six, you could eat it in a different building. I would like a witness who has already seen me at my least impressive. That is a strange offer. You can refuse it."

She did not refuse it. At six-fifteen they walked to a place that served grits and did not hurry grief or joy. Lila changed out of her blazer in the staff room first, so she would arrive as herself. Graham noticed. He did not mention the funeral again until the food was gone, and then he said only that he was glad not to be walking into it from a night of ceiling.

He came back to Charleston a month later for the boat, not for a memorial. He called the hotel and asked for the night auditor's name, and the morning manager gave it because Graham asked like a person and not like a complaint. Lila met him on her night off, which was the correct time for a beginning. They walked the battery. He was no longer a guest. She was no longer on the clock. The chair by the window went back to being a chair. What they kept was the breakfast, repeated in better weather, and a habit of telling the truth before they asked for company. The suit is in a closet in another city. The ledger still balances. So, gradually, do they.

**End.**
