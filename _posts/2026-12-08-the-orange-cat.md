The cat was orange, enormous, and committed to a schedule. Mornings he sat on Fern's step as if he paid the mortgage. Evenings he sat on Gil's wall as if the wall had been built for him. Fern was thirty-six and worked from a table that faced the step. Gil was fifty and built cabinets in a shop behind his house. They had been neighbors for a year and had spoken mostly through the cat, a limitation the cat did not deserve.

Fern broke it on a day when the cat arrived with a cut on one ear.

"Your evening tenant is injured," she said, at Gil's gate. "I am Fern. I have him in a box. I am not asking you to be his father. I am asking you to help me hold him while I look."

"Gil. I can hold a cat. Fifty. The box was a good instinct."

The cut was shallow. The cat was insulted. They cleaned it on Gil's worktable, moving clamps out of the way, and the cat forgave them by the time the water boiled for tea. Fern saw the shop: the smell of wood, the careful chaos, a radio playing a ballgame he was not really following. Gil saw her hands, steady, and the fact that she had come to the gate instead of posting about the cat online as if neighbors were a concept.

"He eats at my place in the morning," Fern said. "I did not want to steal your friend."

"He eats here at night," Gil said. "We are a custody arrangement with no paperwork. You could come with the evening meal sometimes. Thirty-six is welcome. So is any other age you actually are. I guessed wrong and I am correcting it."

"You guessed close enough. I will come tonight."

She came with the food and stayed for a cabinet door that would not hang straight. Her advice was good. Gil took it. The cat supervised from a shelf, ear already better. Over a week the evening meal became a pattern, and the pattern became a conversation that did not require fur to justify it. Fern told him she had moved to Santa Fe after a magazine job collapsed and now designed books for a press that paid slowly and meant well. Gil told her he had an ex-wife in Albuquerque and a respect for the ex-wife, and a house he had finished himself down to the thresholds.

They did not adopt the cat so much as admit he had adopted a pair. The vet visit was shared. The bill was shared. Fern and Gil kept both porches, because the cat's schedule was sacred and because neither of them was ready to collapse two houses into one. Readiness came later, in a practical way: a leak in her roof, a month in his guest room that stopped being guest, a decision made over sink hardware, which is a very Santa Fe way to choose a life. The orange cat now has one porch and one shop shelf and the smugness of a plan that worked. Fern and Gil have the rest. They still thank him, and they know, most days, that they walked through the gate themselves.

**End.**
