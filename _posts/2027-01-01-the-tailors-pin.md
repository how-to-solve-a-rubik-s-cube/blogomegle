Mr. Adeyemi had a pin in his mouth and a bride's sister on the box. The sister was Sasha, twenty-seven, and the dress was not hers. She was standing still because she had been asked to, and because still was easier than the conversation in the next room, where the wedding was being planned at a volume. He was sixty-two. He pinned the hem, said, "Down," and did not ask her when she would be the one in the white. She liked him immediately for the omission.

Oliver came in for a pickup while she was still on the box. He was thirty-six, collecting a suit that had been let out at the shoulder after a year of swimming. He nodded at the tailor, saw the pins, and waited at a distance that did not crowd the work.

"Your jacket is on the hook," Mr. Adeyemi said. "Try the arm before you go. I will be two minutes. This hem is more urgent than your lapel."

Sasha smiled at the ranking. Oliver smiled back, not as a man watching a woman on a box, as a man who had been correctly deprioritized. When she stepped down, the tailor introduced them the way he introduced people who might otherwise orbit and leave.

"Sasha, Oliver. Oliver, Sasha. You both stand well and you both listen. That is the whole speech. I have a shop to run."

They stood on the pavement after, Sasha with a dress in a bag that was not her future, Oliver with a suit that was only a suit.

"He does that," Oliver said. "Puts two customers in a sentence and then abandons them. I am Oliver. If the sentence was unwelcome, the bus stop is a clean exit."

"The sentence was fine," Sasha said. "I am not shopping for a wedding. I am shopping for a cup of tea that is not about a wedding. If you are free from the suit, I know a place that does not have a theme."

The place had no theme. They talked about work, hers in a school office, his in a planning department, and about the tailor, who had altered Oliver's clothes since a first job interview and had never once asked him to narrate his personal progress. Sasha said that was a rare mercy. Oliver said the mercy could be a model. He did not ask her to be a story. He asked if he could see her on a day when nobody needed a hem.

Mr. Adeyemi heard, later, that they had gone for tea. He nodded, pleased and unsurprised, and went back to a sleeve. He is not their matchmaker in the sentimental sense. He is a man who saw two adults standing well and refused to do the work of pretending otherwise. The romance is Sasha and Oliver's: a Tuesday, a flat with a good table, a tailor they still visit separately and sometimes together, for clothes that fit their actual lives. The pin stays in the shop. The choice walked out onto the pavement and kept going.

**End.**
