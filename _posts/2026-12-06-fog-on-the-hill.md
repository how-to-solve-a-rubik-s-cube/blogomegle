The fog was a wall with a street inside it. Ray had driven the route for eight years and still disliked the hill on nights like this, not from fear, from the knowledge that a city asks drivers to see what the air is withholding. He was forty-four. Donna was the last passenger, forty-seven, going home from a shift at a hotel desk. She had pulled the cord for her stop and then, watching the white, had not stood.

"I cannot see the curb," she said. "I would rather ride to the end than step into a guess."

"I am not supposed to stop between," Ray said. "I am going to stop between. Hold the rail. I am Ray."

He stopped where a streetlight made a small coin of visibility. The curb appeared, ordinary and blessed. Donna stepped down and turned.

"Donna. Thank you. If that gets you in trouble, I will say I tripped and you were a humanitarian. Forty-seven. I mean it."

"It will not get me in trouble if you do not write a novel about it. Have a safer night."

She had a safer night. She also had a thought, which was that a person who breaks a rule carefully is a person worth identifying in daylight. The next afternoon, fogless and almost rude with sun, she boarded at the same stop and stood by the driver's window until he looked.

"Daylight," Donna said. "I brought you a coffee that is still hot. If you cannot take it while you drive, I will drink it myself and the gesture will survive."

"I can take it at the layover," Ray said. "Ride to the end of the run. Forty-four. I would like to say the rest without a mirror between us."

At the layover lot, buses ticked as they cooled. Ray drank the coffee. Donna told him she had worked hotels long enough to know the difference between charm and care, and that stopping the bus had been care. He told her he lived with a nephew who was in school and that his schedule was a puzzle he could solve if someone wanted a Tuesday. She wanted a Tuesday. She said so.

Tuesdays became a walk on a flat street, chosen on purpose, no hills, no fog required. Donna learned the nephew's name and his opinion about cereal. Ray learned that Donna had a sister in Oakland and a plan to leave the night desk within a year. He did not offer to drive her life. She did not offer to manage his. They met as two shift workers with a coffee and a good piece of judgment between them.

Ray still stops only where he should, except on the nights the curb disappears, and then he stops where it reappears. Donna is not always on the bus. When she is, she pulls the cord and stands. The exception remains an exception. The Tuesday walk is the rule they wrote themselves, in clear air, where both of them can see the ground.

**End.**
