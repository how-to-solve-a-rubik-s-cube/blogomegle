The hold shelf was not a library romance. It was a metal cart behind a counter in a city archive in Toronto, and the book on it was a manual of stair joints that two people had requested for entirely practical reasons.

Nadine was twenty-five and worked there. Her job was to know where a box had been, not to fall in love with the public, a category she regarded as weather. Carl was forty, a carpenter, and he had ordered the manual because a client wanted a staircase that looked old and would not kill anyone. He gave his name. Nadine pulled the book. A second slip fell out of it, with the same title and a different name: hers. She had requested it that morning for a finding aid and had not yet fetched it.

"We are both on hold," she said. "There is one copy. I can take it after you, or you can take it after me. I need an hour. You may need a week. I am Nadine."

"Carl. Take your hour. I have brought a pencil and a humility I do not always have. Forty, if the hour comes with small talk. I would rather know I am not explaining screws to a teenager."

"Twenty-five. You are not."

She read at the end of the counter, standing, because the archive did not encourage lingering and she did it anyway. Carl waited on the bench that was really for researchers with appointments. He did not stare. He looked at a poster about acid-free folders as if it might contain a plot. When Nadine closed the book she had flagged three pages with paper strips, not because she was keeping them from him, but because the strips were her method.

"The joint on page forty is the one people fake," she said. "The real one is on page forty-four. I am not a carpenter. I am a person who has seen the lawsuit files."

"That is more useful than a carpenter who has not," Carl said.

He took the book. He also took the strips, with her permission, and returned them the next day inside the book, along with a note that said: You were right about forty-four. I cut it that way. The stair will hold. Thank you. If you drink coffee at a time the archive allows, I am buying.

The archive allowed four o'clock. They went to a place across the street that had one window and a great many coats on hooks. Nadine learned he had apprenticed late, after a decade in warehouses, and was proud of the lateness because it meant he had chosen. Carl learned she lived with two roommates and a fern that was trying. He did not call the work romantic. She did not call the stair romantic. The stair was a stair. The coffee was a decision.

He started requesting boxes the proper way, with dates and patience, and once a month he requested nothing and only arrived at four. Nadine liked the difference. She kept her job and her counter and her rules. He kept his pencil. The manual went back to the shelf. The joint on page forty-four is in a house in the east end, holding people up, which is what it was for. The two of them are also holding, in a less structural and equally serious way: a standing hour, a true copy, a man and a woman who shared one book without pretending there were two.
