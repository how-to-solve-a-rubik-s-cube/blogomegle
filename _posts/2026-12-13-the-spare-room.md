The hotels were full in the way of a city that had also scheduled a convention and a game. Holly had a sofa in the bride's childhood room and no complaint, until the bride's childhood room also acquired an air mattress and a cousin of the groom. The cousin's name was Grant. He was thirty-eight. Holly was thirty-five. They were not related to each other, a fact both of them established within the first minute, out of manners and a little hope.

"I can sleep in the car," Grant said. "I have done it. It is not noble. It is mostly a cramp."

"You can sleep on the air mattress," Holly said. "I am a grown woman on a sofa. If you snore I will throw a sock. I am Holly. The bride is my cousin. I am here to hem a dress in the morning and then be charming for six hours."

"The groom is my cousin. I am here to keep an uncle from making a speech about cryptocurrency. We have complementary missions."

The sock was not required. Grant slept like a person who had driven since noon. In the morning Holly burned her wrist on a curling iron and said a word the wedding party pretended not to hear. Grant was already in the kitchen, making coffee that tasted like a decision. He ran cool water and held the sprayer toward her wrist without grabbing her arm.

"You aim," he said. "I will not manage you."

She aimed. The burn was minor. The not-managing stayed with her through the vows and the uncle, who did attempt the speech and was diverted by Grant with a toast so boring it became funny. Holly laughed from the table of honor. Grant, at the back, looked up as if the laugh were a cue he had been waiting for without admitting it.

After the cake, when the band was louder than the feelings, they met by the coat rack.

"I have a room tomorrow," Grant said. "The convention leaves. I would like to see this city when I am not a cousin. If you are free for a bad breakfast, I am asking. If the sofa was enough acquaintance, I will take the no and the coffee as a fair trade."

"Breakfast," Holly said. "I am free. I live here. I can also show you a good one and we can save the bad one for a story."

They chose the good one. Grant extended his drive by a day, then by a ticket change he told her about only after he had done it, so it would be his cost and not her pressure. Holly showed him the river and the shop where she sold other people's guitars and sometimes her own repairs. He could hear that she was good. He said so once. She kept the sentence.

They are past the wedding now. The couple who got married are happy and irrelevant. Holly and Grant keep a route between Nashville and his city, and a plan, slower than a wedding weekend, to pick one kitchen. The air mattress is back in a closet. The spare room, these days, is a guest room in the house they are furnishing on purpose. Nobody is sleeping in a car. The sock remains a joke, unused, which is its best career.

**End.**
