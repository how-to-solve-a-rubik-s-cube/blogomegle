The bus was cold in the way of air conditioning that has never heard of a blanket. Zoe had the window and a sweater she was using as a pillow and a plan to sleep through Colorado. Chris had the aisle and a book he was not reading and legs that did not fit. He was thirty-three. She was twenty-six. The overhead light was the color of a waiting room.

"If I angle out, you can have the armrest," he said. "I am Chris. I am not going to narrate the journey. I would like the armrest decision to be mutual so I do not spend four hours being a thief."

"Zoe. Take the outer half. I will take the inner. If I fall asleep on your shoulder, wake me. I do not volunteer people as furniture."

Neither of them slept. The highway did the long dark thing highways do, and the talking started because the book was bad and the sweater was insufficient. Zoe was moving to Santa Fe for a conservation job at a museum that had more pots than staff. Chris was going back to a house he had grown up in, to help his mother sell it, and he was trying not to make the trip a personality. They traded the facts without turning them into auditions. At Raton the bus stopped long enough for bad coffee. They brought two cups back and the conversation restarted without a ritual.

"I do not believe the universe sat us here," Zoe said. "I believe the ticket was cheap. I am saying that so we do not have to flirt with destiny. We can just be two people who are good at this part."

"Agreed," Chris said. "Cheap tickets are a sufficient origin story."

By the time the mountains took a shape, they knew the important small things. Zoe had a brother who would drive her from the station. Chris had three days of boxes and then a return ticket he could change. He did not change it on the bus, in a surge of plot. He asked, at the station, in the gray morning, whether she would want a dinner that was not served on a tray after the boxes were done. She said Thursday. She wrote the number on the back of his bookmark, because paper was what they had.

Thursday happened. The dinner was green chile and a table that did not recline. Chris met the brother for exactly ten minutes, which everyone survived. Zoe saw the house before it was emptied and did not offer decorating advice. She carried one box because she wanted to, and he let her. The return ticket got changed a week later, honestly, after a conversation about schedules and not about signs.

They live in Santa Fe now, in a rental that smells like new paint and old clay. The overnight bus remains a cheap ticket, not a myth. When they tell it, they include the armrest and the bad coffee and Zoe's refusal of fate. The refusal is why it worked. Two adults kept each other company until the mountains, then made a plan in daylight, when nobody was tired enough to mistake comfort for a vow. The vow came later, in a kitchen, over a lease. It was wide awake.
