The glasses were in a case the color of a plum, which was why Ian saw them on the seat after the carriage emptied. He was fifty-two and he rode the same line every weekday, a commute he had stopped resenting and started using as reading time. The case had a card inside with a name and a work number. Joyce. A surgery on Oxford Road.

He could have handed them to the station office. He called instead, at a reasonable hour, because a person who reads on a train might want the glasses before the next appointment and not after a form.

"This is Ian," he said. "I have a plum-colored case from the eight-twelve. If you are Joyce, I can leave them at the barrier tomorrow, or I can bring them to the desk if the desk is expecting a man with a backpack and no medical complaints."

"I am Joyce. Bring them to the desk. I have been squinting at a screen like a Victorian and I am tired of it. Fifty-nine, so the receptionist does not invent a romance on your behalf. You may invent nothing. Just the glasses."

"Fifty-two. Only the glasses."

He brought them at noon. The receptionist did invent a romance, silently, with her eyebrows. Joyce collected the case and checked the lenses as if he might have substituted a cheaper pair, then looked ashamed of the check and said so.

"Habit," she said. "People lose things and replace them with approximations. These are mine. Thank you. There is a cafe in the building that is terrible. If you have already used your lunch, ignore me."

"I have not used it," Ian said.

The cafe was terrible and the conversation was not. Joyce was a nurse practitioner who had started the job late and loved it with a ferocity she did not soften. Ian managed maintenance for a university and talked about boilers the way some people talk about dogs. She asked an actual question about a boiler. He answered without performing expertise past the point of use. At the end of the hour she put the glasses on to read the bill and he saw her face settle into its proper focus. It was a good face.

"Same train tomorrow," Joyce said. "I will be in the third carriage, not losing anything. If you sit nearby we can complain about the cafe. If you sit far, I will assume you have a life, which you are allowed."

He sat nearby. They complained, then talked about a sister, a strike, a book she was too busy for and was reading anyway. The glasses stayed in the plum case except when needed. Ian began to meet her on purpose, which replaced the myth of the commute as an accident. Months later the case lives on their shared table, and both of them can find it, which is a domestic achievement. The train still runs. Joyce still squints only when she forgets, and Ian still notices before she does, and hands them over without a speech. Some returns do not need a barrier. They need a person who rides the same line and decides to keep doing it beside you.

**End.**
