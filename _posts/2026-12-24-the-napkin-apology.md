They had ended it at thirty and thirty-two, in a parking lot, over a job Malik took in another city and a sentence Tasha could still quote because it had been unfair. He had said she could visit if she wanted it enough. She had wanted a partner, not a commuting prize. She had said so. He had driven off as if the drive were the argument.

Five years later he was in the same Atlanta diner where they had once been happy for cheap reasons, eggs and a booth. He did not know she still went there. She did, on Wednesdays, because the eggs had never been the problem. She saw him before he saw her. She could have left. She sat in his line of sight and let the choice be mutual.

He did not come over with a speech. He came with a napkin, wrote on it at the counter, and brought it as if it were a document.

I was wrong about enough. I am in town through Friday. If you want nothing, fold this and I will go. Malik.

Tasha read it. She was thirty-five. He was thirty-seven. The napkin had a grease spot and no excuses.

"Sit," she said. "You will not get a reunion. You might get a conversation. Start with the job. Did it at least turn out to be the job?"

"It was the job," Malik said. "It was not worth the sentence. I have rehearsed better ones and I am not going to perform them at you. I am sorry. I live in Atlanta again. I am not asking to move back into a life I left. I am asking whether we can eat eggs and see who is actually here."

They ate. Tasha told him about a school she now ran operations for, and about a nephew. Malik told her about the other city and a loneliness he had earned. He did not ask to be forgiven for entertainment. He asked what her Wednesday looked like next week. She said it looked like this diner, and he could come if he arrived without a napkin, because one document was enough.

He arrived the next Wednesday with nothing in his hands. That was the apology she believed. They rebuilt slowly, in public, in a booth, then in a park, then in a kitchen where the rules were said out loud: no job would be a verdict on the other's wanting, and no drive-off would count as a period. The napkin is in a book at Tasha's house, not framed, just kept, grease spot and all. Malik knows where it is. He does not need to read it. He stayed, the first time and the times after, which was the whole repair.

**End.**
