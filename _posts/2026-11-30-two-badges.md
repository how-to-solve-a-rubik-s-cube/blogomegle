The private tour ended at nine, and the visitors left with the particular gratitude of people who have been allowed into a room after the sign said no. Elena was thirty-four and had given the tour. James was thirty-nine and had stood by the cases, not because the visitors were dangerous, but because the objects were, in the delicate way of wood and pigment. Their badges said the same museum and different departments.

"I will start at the far gallery," James said. "You take the lights. If we meet in the middle we are on time."

"If we meet in the middle we might also talk," Elena said. "I am willing. I am Elena. The tour voice is off."

"James. The guard voice was never that on."

They locked the rooms in order. A marble floor made their steps honest. In the middle gallery, a painting of a harbor waited in the dark with one security light on it, which felt like more attention than some people get. Elena paused. James waited without filling the pause.

"I studied this one in school," she said. "I thought I would feel more when I finally had the keys. I feel the same. I like that. It means the painting was never about my access."

"Good," James said. "Access is a job. Liking a thing can be a life."

They finished the round and stepped into the night air of the Mall, which was wide and slightly theatrical and also just a place where buses ran. Elena's badge was still on. She took it off at the sidewalk, because she had a rule about not being staff in her own evening. James did the same, a second later, having watched the rule and adopted it.

"There is a cart that sells arepas until ten," he said. "I am going. Thirty-nine, so you know I am not a story you tell about a younger guard. If you want salt and a walk toward the Metro, I am in no hurry."

"Thirty-four. I want the salt."

They ate standing up, napkins failing, and laughed at the failure. Elena lived in a group house with a cat that belonged to no one and therefore to the house. James lived alone and built model boats badly on purpose, because perfection was what the museum required and he needed a hobby that forgave him. She asked to see a bad boat. He said Friday, and that she could leave whenever the badness stopped being funny.

Friday the boat was lopsided and the tea was good. Elena stayed past the funny part and into the part where he told her about a brother overseas and she told him about a degree she had finished at night. The badges stayed in their bags. At work they still met in the middle gallery and did the job. After work they did the other thing, which had a cart, a Metro, a lopsided boat, and no visitors. The paintings remained themselves. So did Elena and James, which was the condition that let them choose each other without asking the museum to bless it.

**End.**
