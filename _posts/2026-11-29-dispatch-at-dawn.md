The headset lived on a hook between their chairs, and the rule was simple: whoever was talking to the trucks wore it, and the other one wrote. Keisha was twenty-nine and had been on nights for a year. Ron was forty and had been on nights long enough to know which drivers called calm when they were not.

Kansas City at four in the morning was a grid of lights and one diner that never fully committed to closing. A refrigerated truck broke down on an overpass. Keisha took the call. Ron pulled the map they were not supposed to need anymore, because the screen had frozen, because screens pick their moments.

"Tell him mile marker twelve has a shoulder that is a lie," Ron said. "Send him back to eleven. I will call the wrecker."

She told the driver. Her voice stayed level. When the wrecker was rolling, she put the headset on the hook and her hands on the desk so they would stop shaking in private.

"You were good," Ron said.

"I was trained," Keisha said. "Do not make it a character moment. I am Keisha. I already know your name because it is on the board. I would like breakfast when this shift is over, and I would like it not to be a reward for a crisis."

"Ron. Forty. Breakfast is available without a crisis. Twenty-nine looks right on you, for what that is worth, and I will not say it again if it landed wrong."

"It landed fine. Say things once. I keep them."

They went to the diner at seven, still in fleeces that smelled like the office coffee. The waitress knew Ron and did not know Keisha and fixed that by the second cup. Keisha ate pancakes as a serious project. Ron ordered eggs and then stole nothing from her plate, a restraint she filed under promising. They talked about a sister of hers who thought night work was a phase, and a son of his who lived with his mother and played trumpet badly and with joy. The custody was amicable. Keisha did not flinch at the word son. Ron noticed the not-flinching and was careful not to look relieved in a way that would make her responsible for it.

Breakfast became the end of every shared shift, and then the point of requesting the same rotation. They were good at the job together. They were also good at leaving it on the hook. Keisha insisted on that. Ron agreed. At the diner they did not replay calls unless someone had been unkind, and then they replayed only long enough to decide what to do. Most mornings they talked about a garden Ron was failing on a balcony and a class Keisha was taking in logistics, which she was going to finish, which he believed without supervising.

They still share the headset and the hook and the diner. The love is not that they handle emergencies. Plenty of people handle emergencies and go home alone. The love is the booth afterward, the ordinary food, the way he says a thing once and she keeps it, and the way both of them clock out all the way.

**End.**
