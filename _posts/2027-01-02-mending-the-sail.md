The sail had a tear the shape of a shrug. Peg spread it on the floor of Walt's loft and tried to look like a person who had meant to own a boat. She was fifty-one. The boat had belonged to a neighbor who was moving inland and had sold it for a price that was really a request: please use it. Walt was fifty-six and had been mending sails since his hands were large enough to pull a needle. He did not laugh at the tear.

"You can pay me," he said, "or you can learn the stitch and pay me less. Learning takes the afternoon. Paying takes ten minutes. Both are respectable. I am Walt."

"Teach me the stitch," Peg said. "I am Peg. I bought the boat so I would have to leave the house. If I only pay you, I will have left the house once."

They sat on the floor. The needle was awkward and then less so. Walt was a good teacher in the specific way: he let her make a crooked inch and then showed her how to see it, without taking the work back. Peg had run a town clerk's office and was used to being competent. The inch of incompetence was a gift she had not known she needed. She said that, briefly. He nodded and kept sewing.

By evening the tear was a seam. The loft smelled like wax and salt. Walt made tea because his hands needed a different job. Peg looked at the harbor, which was right there, doing its job without her.

"Come out on it tomorrow," she said. "Not as a lesson. As a person who knows why the sail is a sail. If I am a hopeless captain you can say so and we will come back in. Fifty-one. I would rather be hopeless in company than competent on a couch."

"Fifty-six. I will come. I am rarely hopeless and often cold. Bring a second coat."

They went out. She was not hopeless. She was new, which is a different condition, and he treated it as such. The boat, whose name was already painted and slightly foolish, took the wind like a fact. Peg laughed once, a real laugh, and Walt filed the sound under reasons to be on the water.

They use the boat in good weeks and the loft in bad ones. The seam has held. Peg learned other stitches. Walt learned her town, her sister, her way of going quiet when she is content. The couch is no longer the destination. A second coat hangs on his hook and on hers, interchangeable, because the weather is shared. She bought a boat to leave the house. She found a person who would leave it with her and still be glad to come back to the same dock.

**End.**
