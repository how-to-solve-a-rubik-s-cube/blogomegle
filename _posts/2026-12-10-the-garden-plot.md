The allotments were divided by a path and a low wire that would not stop a determined dog or a conversation. Nuala's tomatoes were earlier. She was sixty-three and treated this as a responsibility rather than a boast. Seamus's tomatoes were late and philosophical. He was sixty-seven and pretended not to compare. The pretending lasted until July, when he leaned on the wire and said, "Yours have the look of a plan. Mine have the look of a hope. What did you do in May that I was too proud to ask about?"

"Compost and a bit of fleece on the cold nights," Nuala said. "I am Nuala. I can hand you fleece without a lecture. I used to be a midwife. Lectures are a professional hazard I am trying to retire."

"Seamus. I used to drive a bus. I am used to routes. A tomato that will not follow one is a personal insult. I accept the fleece."

She handed it over the wire. Their fingers did not quite behave. Neither of them apologized, which would have made it smaller. Seamus applied the fleece that night and came back in the morning to report, formally, that the plants looked less offended. Nuala invited him around the path to see a bed of beans she was willing to be proud of. He came around. Standing on the same side of the wire was a different geography.

They began to share a thermos on Sundays. Seamus's wife had died four years ago, and he said her name, Maureen, while tying a vine, so the name would be part of the garden and not a shadow outside it. Nuala's husband was alive and in Cork and not her husband anymore, a sentence she delivered once, cleanly. Seamus nodded and passed the thermos. The tomatoes improved. So did the hour.

In August she cut a gate in the wire, a ridiculous and perfect piece of work, and showed him how the latch worked.

"You can still go the long way," she said. "The gate is for when you do not want to."

"I do not want to," Seamus said.

They use the gate. The plots remain two plots, because a person needs a row that is theirs, and because love in later life works better with a boundary that opens rather than disappears. On Sundays the thermos sits on either side, depending on the weather and the weeds. Neighbors assume they are a couple and are correct. The tomatoes do not care who started them. Nuala and Seamus care who waters them, and the answer, now, is whichever of them reaches the tap first, and then the other, always, with a second cup.

**End.**
