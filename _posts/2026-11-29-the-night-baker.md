The street was blue at four-thirty, the color Quebec City keeps for people who have not been to bed. Camille had. She had slept from eight until eleven and then come downstairs to the ovens, which was her arrangement with the day. She was thirty-two. The first loaves came out at five, and the bell over the door was not supposed to ring until six.

It rang at five-oh-two. Marc stood in a coat that had worked a night shift of its own. He was thirty-six, a translator who preferred the hours when emails were asleep.

"I know you are not open," he said. "I could smell the bread from the corner and I have a train at six-forty. If the loaf is for sale, I will be quick. If it is not, I will leave and think well of you."

"It is for sale," Camille said. "I am Camille. The early bell is a privilege, not a habit. Do not bring friends."

"Marc. I have very few friends awake at this hour. They would not survive the introduction."

He paid with exact change and ate the heel on the step, because the train did not require him to be elegant. Camille watched through the glass and made a decision she did not announce. When he came back the next week, same coat, same almost-apology, she had a cup on the counter.

"Coffee is not on the menu at five," she said. "It is on the house if you tell me whether the heel was any good."

"It was the best thing in a bad week," Marc said. "I am thirty-six. I translate manuals for ships. The bread was more fluent."

They made the early bell a private opening. Not every day. On the days his train, or later his deadlines, put him on the street, he knocked once and waited, so she could pretend she had not heard if the dough was at a cruel stage. She never pretended. He learned the difference between a loaf that needed five more minutes and a woman who needed the door shut, and he treated those as separate facts.

Camille told him she had chosen nights because mornings in a shared bakery had meant men explaining her own starter to her. Marc told her he had left an office job because the fluorescent lights made him feel filed. Neither confession was a wound they asked the other to heal. They were context. The context grew a breakfast: two stools, butter, the end of a loaf that was not quite straight. When the sun finally cleared the opposite roof, Marc washed his cup without being asked.

By spring he had a key to the side door, given, not found. He still knocked. The knock was the part she had not known she wanted, a man who could enter and still requested the moment. The first loaf is still his if he is there. If he is not, it goes in the window like any other. The love is not the bread. The love is that both of them reorganized their sleep around a bell, and neither of them calls the other difficult for needing the night.

**End.**
