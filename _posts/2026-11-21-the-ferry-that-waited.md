The ramp was already up when Nina reached it. She was thirty-one, her lanyard was still in her hand from a meeting that had used the word circulate too often, and the ferry made the sound of a large animal deciding to leave.

Owen was thirty-six and on the deck side of the gate. He was not the captain. He was the person who could still reach the latch. He looked at her, at the gap, at the clock the company cared about, and held the gate.

"One minute," he said. "Do not make me a legend. I am Owen. Run."

She ran. The gap was not heroic. It was a step. Her shoe scuffed the plate. A passenger sighed in the key of people who have already suffered. Nina stood in the cabin with her pulse in her ears and found him by the door, writing the minute into a log as if a minute were a thing that could be stored.

"Thank you," she said. "I am Nina. Thirty-one. I will not tell anyone you are soft."

"Thirty-six. Tell them I am slow at latches. That scans better."

The crossing was forty minutes of gray water and a window that needed washing. They did not sit together. She had a seat. He had a job. Once, when the boat leaned, he put a hand out toward a sliding cup and did not touch the woman holding it, only the table. Nina saw that. She collected small evidences the way other people collected stamps.

At the terminal she waited until he was off the clock, which she could tell because the high-visibility vest came off and he became a man with a lunch bag. She did not want the debt of the minute to become a scene.

"I owe you a coffee that is not from a ferry," she said. "Tomorrow, if you are on the early boat. I will be the person not running."

"I am on the early boat," Owen said. "I will be the person with the latch and the ordinary face."

She was there. She was not running. They drank coffee from a shop that opened before the light did, sitting on a bench that faced the water and also, less romantically, a row of bins. Nina edited reports for a fisheries office. Owen had worked the boats since he was old enough to be trusted with a rope, and he was precise about that age because he disliked myths of born sailors. He was a man who had learned a job. She was a woman who had missed a boat and then decided missing it once was enough.

They kept the early coffee twice a week. On the other days she made the ramp with time to spare, and he still looked up, and the looking was no longer a rescue. It was recognition. The company never heard about the minute. The log stayed boring. What changed was a bench, a vest on a hook, and two adults who agreed that being on time for each other was a better story than being late for a ship.
