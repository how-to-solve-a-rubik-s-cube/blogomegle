The gallery closed at six. Adele knew this because she was the person who said so. She was fifty-two, and the radio on her belt made a noise like a polite insect. Frank was fifty-five and was still in the back room with a frame that had arrived cracked, which was not the gallery's fault and was now, inconveniently, his.

"I need ten minutes," he said.

"You have eight," Adele said. "I am not being unkind. The alarm believes in eight."

He worked with his sleeves rolled and his glasses on his head, which meant he could not find them, which meant Adele pointed. He thanked her as if she had done something rarer than looking. The picture was a field in winter, mostly gray, with one red shed that justified the trip. Frank set the new frame around it and stepped back.

"Better," Adele said.

"You are allowed to say it is the same."

"I do not waste sentences."

They had spoken before, in the way of people who share a building and a set of keys. This was the first time the building was empty. Denver outside the glass was already dark, the mountains a rumor. Adele walked the rooms and turned the lights down in order. Frank carried the crate to the door and waited where she could see him, because he had learned, in other jobs, that a man lingering in a closing building is a problem until he is a known problem.

At the door she locked the top bolt and then the bottom. He held the crate and did not offer to take her keys. She liked that. Help, in her experience, often arrived as a hand on something she was already holding.

"There is a diner two blocks down that will still be serving eggs," Frank said. "I am going. If you are going the same direction, we can complain about the crate. If you are not, I will complain to the eggs."

"I am going that direction," Adele said. "I live past it. I am fifty-two, in case the badge light made me look like a rumor."

"Fifty-five. The glasses were a clue."

The diner had a booth with a tear in the seat that both of them avoided without discussing it. They ate eggs. They talked about a son of his who built trails, and a sister of hers who believed Denver was a phase Adele had been having since 1998. Frank said he had been married, and that it had ended in a kitchen, quietly, over whose turn it was to call the plumber. Adele said she had not been married, and that this was not a riddle. He nodded and passed the pepper.

They began to leave the gallery together on the nights his deliveries ran late. Not every night. Adele still had a route and a radio and a preference for doing her job without an audience. Frank learned the nights. He brought nothing but his own finished work and the willingness to be outside by six-oh-eight. She started saving him the true count of minutes. He started arriving with the glasses already on his face.

What they have now is a booth, a standing order, and a set of keys that remain hers. The lights still go off in order. The difference is that someone waits where she can see him, and they walk toward eggs as if that were a reasonable way to begin a life.

**End.**
