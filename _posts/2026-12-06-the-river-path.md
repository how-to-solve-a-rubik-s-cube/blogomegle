Sandra walked toward the bridge. Bill walked away from it. They passed each other at roughly seven-thirty on weekdays, and the passing had acquired a nod so consistent it was almost a grievance when one of them missed it. She was fifty-eight and walked because her doctor had been specific and because she liked the river more than she liked the doctor. He was sixty-one and walked because the house was too quiet after his retirement and the quiet had started to feel like a verdict.

On a morning when the path was iced in patches, they both stopped at the same patch, and the nod had nowhere to go.

"It is slick at the edge," Sandra said. "I am Sandra. I am going to the bridge and back. If you fall, I will not enjoy being right."

"Bill. I am going to the point and back. I accept the warning. Sixty-one. You may ignore my advice in return. It keeps the balance."

"Fifty-eight. The balance is noted."

They did not walk together that day. They did the next, because Bill turned around, frankly and without drama, and said, "I would rather have a conversation than a route." Sandra considered her route, which was sacred in the small way of health habits, and decided the habit was walking, not solitude. They went toward the bridge at her pace.

Bill had run a print shop. His hands still had a nick of ink in one crack, a detail Sandra liked because it proved a life of objects. She had managed a school cafeteria and was used to people underestimating the logistics of feeding. He did not underestimate it. He asked how many trays, and listened to the number. She asked what he printed, and he said menus, mostly, and wedding programs, and he did not make a face at either.

They began to walk the same direction three mornings a week. The other two, they kept their opposite routes, a preservation of self that both of them understood as care rather than distance. On Fridays they added a bakery that did not mind sweaty coats. Sandra told him she had been widowed for six years and did not want a man who was looking for a nurse. Bill said he was looking for a person who would tell him when the path was slick. He could make his own soup. He demonstrated this on a Sunday, in his kitchen, which was clean and a little sad and improved by her coat on the chair.

The river still runs both ways, in the sense that they let it. Some mornings she has the bridge to herself. Some mornings he does. The mornings they meet are agreed in a note on her fridge and a note on his, the same time, the same bench if the weather turns. They are not joggers. They never were. They are two adults who stopped treating a nod as the maximum courtesy and found, in the extra mile, a life with two kitchens and one direction they choose.

**End.**
