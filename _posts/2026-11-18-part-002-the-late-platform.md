The train should not have stopped in Astoria. Nora had chosen the route because it did not stop there. She was twenty-nine, on her way to a night shift interview two towns up the coast, and she had spent five years arranging her life so she would not have to stand on the platform where she had returned a ring.

Elliot had been thirty-one then. He had taken a dispatch job inland, a better wage, a windowless room, a future he could describe in numbers. Nora was a nurse in her father's small clinic and would not leave the night roster. They were both old enough to sign a lease and old enough to be stubborn. They broke the engagement under the platform lamp. No one shouted. Elliot said he could not ask her to shrink her work. Nora said he already had, by buying one ticket. She kept the ring in her coat for a week and then mailed it, because handing it back had felt like a scene and she was tired of scenes.

She was twenty-four when they promised, twenty-four when they stopped. She did not look for him. He did not write. People who part cleanly sometimes do it so well that the years have nothing to argue with.

The signal failed at 9:40 p.m. The conductor said twenty minutes, maybe thirty. Passengers stepped down to stretch. Nora stayed in her seat until she saw, through the glass, a man in a transit vest set a lantern on the same post. He was thirty-four now if she was counting right. The vest was new. The way he checked the platform edge with his eyes, and not with a speech, was not new.

She got off because sitting still felt like hiding, and she had not come here to hide. She had come here by accident, which is the only way some people can be brave.

"Nora," he said, as if the name had been waiting behind his teeth.

"You work the stop you left," she said.

"They moved the desk back to the coast last spring. I asked." He looked at the lantern, not at her coat. "I did not know you would be on a train that does not stop."

"It stopped."

They had eighteen minutes by the announcement board. Elliot told her the inland job had paid for his mother's surgery and then ended when the office closed. He had come home without a plan and taken the night platform because he knew the town's timetable by heart. Nora told him her father had retired, the clinic had been folded into a larger practice, and the night roster she had refused to abandon now belonged to someone else. She was interviewing up the coast for a ward that needed a nurse who did not panic at 2 a.m. She was not interviewing for him. She said that out loud, so the platform could hear it.

"I know," Elliot said. "I am not a reason. I am a man who should have bought two tickets, or none."

The conductor called ten minutes. People drifted back toward the doors. Nora felt the old hurry, the wish to end a feeling before it became a decision. She also felt that she was twenty-nine, not a passenger in someone else's story, and that a signal failure was not fate. It was a fault in a wire. What she did with the minutes was hers.

She told the conductor she would take the morning train. She had an address for the interview and a phone. Missing one night's travel would not erase her name from the list. Elliot did not touch her. He picked up the lantern so another train could pass, did his job, and then walked her as far as the diner by the station, which had started opening late for crews.

They ate eggs at a window that faced the tracks. He did not ask her to stay in Astoria. She did not offer to cancel the interview. He asked if he could call after her shift decision, whatever the decision was. She said yes, and she said the number once, slowly, the way you read a timetable to someone who has missed a stop before.

In the morning she took the train that was supposed to stop. He was on the platform with the lantern unlit, because it was daylight. He lifted a hand. She lifted hers. The happy ending, on that day, was only this: both of them remained, and neither of them had to pretend the other had vanished when the cars began to move.
