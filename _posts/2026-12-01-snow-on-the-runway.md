The storm closed the runway at eleven, which Ann had predicted and operations had hoped against. She was thirty-eight and had already walked her passengers back into the terminal with the voice she used for facts. Viktor was forty-one and was sitting in the de-icing truck with the heater on and the boom folded, because there is no point spraying a plane that is not going anywhere.

He saw her cross the pad with her hood up and no interest in being dramatic. He opened the passenger door.

"You can wait in the building," he said. "Or you can wait here, where the radio works and the coffee is an insult. I am Viktor."

"Ann. I have seen the building. I will take the insult. Thirty-eight. I fly the short routes. Tonight I fly none of them."

"Forty-one. I spray the planes so you can. Tonight I spray none of them. We are a pair of canceled verbs."

The truck was bright inside, a small room of switches. Snow stacked on the windshield and slid when the heater won. They talked because the alternative was listening to a frequency full of people saying the same thing. Ann had a sister in Juneau and a rule about not dating inside the crew. Viktor was not crew in the way the rule meant. He was a contractor with a union card and a careful hands. She revised nothing out loud. She filed the distinction.

He told her about a son who lived with him half the month and loved the truck more than the airplane, which Viktor pretended to mind. Ann told him she had wanted the long routes and then discovered she liked seeing the same mountains often enough to notice when a ridge changed. The coffee lived down to its reputation. They drank it.

"When this lifts," Viktor said, "you will be busy and I will be spraying, and this will have been a pocket. If you want the pocket to have a sequel that is not a storm, I eat at the cafeteria at five on days I am not with my kid. You can sit down. You can also wave and keep walking. Both will be clear."

"I do not wave," Ann said. "I sit or I do not. I will sit on Thursday."

Thursday was clear and viciously cold. She sat. He had saved the chair with his hat, which he removed as soon as she arrived so the saving would not become a scene. They ate chili. They did not recount the snow as if it had been a movie. They planned a Saturday when the son would be with his mother, and Ann could come to a rink and be bad at skating in front of a child who would forgive her because children are practical. She went. She was bad. The boy showed her how to fall without using her hands. Viktor watched them both with a face he did not tidy up.

They are still in Anchorage, still subject to weather, still careful about the job. The truck is for work. The cafeteria is for the sequel. Ann flies the short routes. Viktor sprays. On the nights the runway closes, she knows which bright cab is his, and she does not always go to it, because a pocket is not a right. When she does, he opens the door. The coffee has not improved. They drink it anyway, which is as good a definition of choosing someone as either of them needs.

**End.**
