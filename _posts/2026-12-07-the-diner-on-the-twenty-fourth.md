Buffalo had refused snow, which felt like a personal slight on the twenty-fourth of December. The diner stayed open because Rita had told the owner she would take it, and because some people needed eggs more than they needed a story about family. She was forty. Hank came in at eight, as he did three nights a week, and ordered the usual without looking at the board. He was fifty-three.

"You are allowed to be elsewhere," Rita said, pouring the coffee before he asked.

"I am elsewhere every other day," Hank said. "This is the place. My sister is in Florida. My friends are with their children. I like the children. I like them at a volume this diner does not reach."

The other tables emptied by nine. Rita flipped the sign and did not lock the door. Hank noticed and started to stand, gathering the polite exit. She shook her head.

"Eat the pie," she said. "I am not hosting a charity. I am forty and I do not want to wipe this counter and go up to a television. If you stay, wash a fork. Fifty-three-year-old men can wash forks."

"We can," Hank said. "I was not sure I had been invited or audited."

"Invited."

They washed the small things and left the rest, a rebellion Rita enjoyed more than she expected. Hank had driven a snowplow for the county until his back objected, and now he dispatched the plows from a room with radios. The irony of a snowless Christmas was not lost on him. He spent it carefully, one joke only. Rita had a son in the army who had called at six and been cheerful and brief. She put the briefness on the counter between them and Hank did not try to stretch it. He said, "He called. That is a fact. Facts can be enough for a night."

After the forks they sat in the booth farthest from the window. The street was wet, not white. Rita told him she had been divorced so long the word felt historical. Hank told her he had never married and was done apologizing for the shape of his life. She said she had not asked for an apology. She had asked for a fork washer. He said he could be that on other nights that were not holidays, if she wanted a person and not a season.

January proved it. He came on ordinary Tuesdays, ordered the usual, and stayed to wipe the same three forks, a ritual so plain it could not be mistaken for a performance. In February she went to his apartment, which was neat and had a scanner still murmuring in the corner, and she asked him to turn it down, and he did, immediately, which mattered. The twenty-fourth remains the night they started. The snow arrived late that year, in a sullen heap. They did not require it. They had already chosen each other in the rain.
