The short way was the state road, salted and efficient. The long way was a covered bridge that smelled like dry timber and someone's old hay. Edith took it on evenings when the clinic had been loud. She was sixty-two, a nurse practitioner, and she walked to hear her own steps. George took it because his house sat on the far side and the bridge was simply how a person got there, if the person was not in a hurry. He was sixty-five.

They passed each other for weeks, nods only, the bridge too narrow for a conversation and too dark for pretending not to see. On a Tuesday in mud season they arrived at the middle at the same time, and one of them had to yield, and neither did, and the absurdity made Edith laugh.

"We are a traffic incident," she said. "I am Edith. I come this way to be slower. I can go back."

"George. I come this way because it is the way. You do not have to go back. We can walk to the end together and then be strangers with better data."

They walked. Their shoulders almost touched and did not. George said he had retired from plowing other people's driveways and now plowed only a few, the ones who paid in pie or in nothing, depending on the winter. Edith said she was not retired and might never be, because the clinic was understaffed and she was stubborn. He said stubbornness was a public utility. She accepted the phrase.

At the end of the bridge the road split. His house showed a porch light. Her rented room was a mile the other way, over the efficient road.

"Tomorrow," George said, "if you are on the bridge at six, I will be the man with the slower left knee. If you want the mile to be less alone, I can walk you to the turn and come back. I have the time. I am offering the time, not a rescue."

"I will be there at six," Edith said. "I am sixty-two. Bring nothing. I have had enough of men who bring a solution."

He brought nothing except himself, on time. They did this until the boards of the bridge were a calendar. Edith started keeping a second mug in his kitchen before she called it that. George started sitting in the clinic parking lot on the worst days, not to collect her, to be a fact at the end of the shift if she wanted a fact. Sometimes she drove home the short way and called him from the room. Sometimes she walked. The choice stayed hers, which was the only way she could love a near neighbor.

They live, now, with one porch light and one rented room let go. The bridge is still the long way, and they still take it, even in the car, windows down, timber smell in the vents. The short road remains for ambulances and for people in a hurry. Edith and George are not in a hurry. They already arrived at the middle once, by accident, and every crossing since has been on purpose.
