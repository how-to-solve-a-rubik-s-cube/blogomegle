The receipt was for eggs, bread, and a bunch of dill. On the back, in a hand that had stopped trying to be pretty, Dot had written a soup: onion, potato, the dill, too much pepper, a parmesan rind if you have one, and the instruction do not hurry the onion. She was sixty-three. She had meant to give the receipt to her sister and had instead left it on a bench outside the market in Providence, under an orange that kept it from blowing.

Walter was sixty-six and sat on the bench because his knee preferred a pause before the hill. He read the recipe because it was there. He cooked it that night because his apartment was quiet and a task with an order is a kindness a person can do for himself. The onion got its time. The soup was better than his usual. He wanted to say so to the handwriting.

The market manager knew Dot, because Dot had worked the flower stand on Fridays for a decade and still came for the dill. Walter left the pot, washed, with a note: I followed your onions. I am Walter. Sixty-six. I live on Hope Street. If the recipe was private, I apologize and I still thank you. If it was not, I will be at the bench on Friday with the pot.

Dot was suspicious of men who returned cookware as a gesture. She went anyway, because the note was specific, and specificity is harder to fake than charm. Walter was on the bench with the pot and two spoons in a paper napkin, which he had debated and then included.

"I am not auditioning," he said. "The soup was good. I had no one to tell. That seemed like a small problem with a local solution."

"Dot. Sixty-three. The recipe is my mother's, and she would have hated the parmesan rind, so I added it after she died. You may keep that version."

They ate the last of a second batch from the pot, right there, which was slightly ridiculous and exactly right. Walter had been a bus mechanic and was now a man who fixed friends' radios for the price of the parts. Dot had taught third grade and missed the noise more than the meetings. Both of them had been married. Both marriages had ended in death, not in a story they wanted to tell on a bench. They said the names once, clearly, and then talked about dill.

Friday became the bench and then Dot's kitchen, where the light was better. Walter brought bread and did not rearrange her spoons. Dot learned he took his coffee too weak and did not correct it into a project. He learned she kept the radio on for the talk, not the music, and he started staying through the program. The receipt is on the fridge, orange-stained, the onions still instructed not to hurry. They do not hurry them. They do not hurry the rest, either. The pot has a place in both apartments, which is as close to a vow as either of them required.

**End.**
