The cake was a single slice because the bakery sold slices and Minji had not ordered a whole life. She was twenty-four. It was her birthday. She had told no one at the office, a choice she was defending against her own sentimentality at a tiny table by the window. Jun had the other tiny table. He was twenty-eight, a civil engineer who ate lunch late to avoid the queue and the conversations the queue required.

The bakery had one fork left in the jar. The server looked at the slice and at the two tables and did a small piece of ethics.

"I can wash another," the server said.

"Or we can share the crisis," Jun said, to Minji, not to the room. "I am Jun. I do not need your cake. I need a fork eventually. If it is your birthday, which your face is suggesting and I may be wrong, I will wait."

"It is my birthday," Minji said. "The face is accurate. Sit. I am Minji. I did not want a party. I wanted this slice and a little less loneliness than the last hour. That is a precise order. You can help or you can wait for the washed fork. Both are polite."

He sat. They used the one fork in turns, which was slightly absurd and better than a speech. Minji worked in a city office that approved signs. Jun built the stations the signs were about. They discovered this and laughed at the smallness of the overlap. She did not tell him a tragic reason for the empty birthday. There was not one. There was a year of being new in a team that socialized in a dialect of jokes she had not learned, and a decision to buy herself cake. He treated that as complete.

"I am free at seven on a day that is not your birthday," Jun said. "We could eat food that is not sweet and use two forks from the start. If the charm of this was the shortage, we can let it be a good shortage and stop."

"The charm was that you asked before you sat," Minji said. "Seven on Thursday. I will be twenty-four all year. You do not have to keep celebrating it."

Thursday had two forks and a stew. Jun was on time. Minji brought a book she did not read. They talked about a sign he thought was in the wrong place and she had the pleasure of telling him the form he would need to move it. Bureaucracy as flirtation is an acquired taste. They acquired it.

They are still in Seoul, still busy, still capable of eating alone. They do not always. The birthday slice is an annual joke, purchased on purpose, shared with two forks now because the shortage is over and the company is not. Minji's team has become kinder. She did not need it to, in the end. She needed one person who could hear a precise order — a little less loneliness, not a party — and fill exactly that, no more until she asked.

**End.**
