The speeches had entered their third unnecessary story. Fiona put her napkin on her plate and made the face of a person who has been polite for the exact right amount of time. She was thirty-one. Alistair, two tables over, was already standing, forty-two, a friend of the groom who had fulfilled the friend contract and wanted a train more than he wanted cake.

They met at the cloakroom, both slightly sheepish, both holding number tickets.

"If we leave now we make the ten-forty," Alistair said. "I am not proposing a conspiracy. I am saying the door is behind you and the station is a twelve-minute walk. I am Alistair."

"Fiona. Walk. I love them. I do not love the story about the stag night. I have a heel that will slow us. Do not heroically offer to carry me."

"I will offer a slower pace, which is less cinematic and more useful."

They made the train with four minutes and a shared irritation that turned into a better mood as soon as the carriage was quiet. The wedding had been lovely at five o'clock and unbearable at nine, a transformation both of them had seen before. Fiona worked in housing policy and had a Monday meeting she refused to attend tired. Alistair taught architecture and had a class of first-years who would know if he was bluffing. They sat across a table. The dark countryside did the moving.

"I was glad to see you stand up," Fiona said. "I did not want to be the only person who had a limit."

"Limits are a public service," Alistair said. "If you want a tea at the station rather than a vanished evening, I am buying. If you want the taxi line alone, I will not experience it as a wound."

"Tea. My heel and I accept."

The station tea was too strong and exactly the right ending. Alistair walked her to a taxi and did not negotiate the ride. He asked whether she ever ate lunch near the parliament, a question specific enough to be real. She did. Thursdays. He appeared the next Thursday with a sandwich and a willingness to leave after forty minutes if her meeting demanded it. The meeting demanded thirty. He left at thirty, which was the moment she decided to see him on a weekend, when time was not a corridor.

Someone else's wedding is on a shelf in a photograph they are both slightly in, background, already looking toward a door. Their own plans are less decorated. A flat with good light for drawing. A calendar that respects Mondays. A heel that still complains, and a pace that adjusted and stayed adjusted. They did not need to stay for cake to prove they could celebrate. They celebrated by knowing when to stand up, and by taking the same train on purpose ever since.

**End.**
