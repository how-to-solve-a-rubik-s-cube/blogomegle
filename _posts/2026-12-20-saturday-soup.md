The pot was large enough to bathe a small decision in. Connie had stirred it since seven. She was fifty-five, a retired bus driver, and she volunteered on Saturdays because a route had taught her what a hot meal did for a person at the end of one. Earl dried trays. He was sixty-one, new to the rotation, careful with his hands, which had fixed presses for a newspaper that no longer existed.

They did not speak much while the line was moving. Speaking was for the people eating. After, the room exhaled. Most volunteers went toward the coffee. Connie started the pot. Earl picked up a second sponge without being assigned.

"You do not have to," Connie said.

"I know," Earl said. "I am Earl. I do not like a pot sitting dirty while I pretend I have finished. If you want the sink alone, I can find another chore."

"Connie. Stay. The pot is a two-person animal."

They washed it. Water went up their wrists. Earl told a short story about a press that had eaten a headline, and Connie told one about a passenger who had paid in quarters for ten years and then one day paid with a cake. Neither story asked for pity. At the end the pot shone in the ugly overhead light, which was as close to glory as stainless steel gets.

"Same Saturday?" Earl asked.

"I am here either way," Connie said. "If you are here, take the sponge and not the coffee until the pot is done. That is the whole invitation. Fifty-five. I am not a riddle."

"Sixty-one. I like an invitation with a task in it."

Saturdays accumulated. Earl learned the names Connie already knew, and did not perform knowing them faster than he did. Connie learned he lived alone with a radio and a daughter who called on Sundays. She had a husband who had left a decade ago and a house she had kept. They began a walk after the pot, around the block only, coats open, steam still in their sleeves. The walk grew a second block, then a diner that was not the kitchen, where someone served them and the novelty made them shy and pleased.

They are not a dramatic rescue of each other or of the city. The line still forms. The pot still needs two people. Connie and Earl are those people, and afterward they are two people at a smaller table, paying for their own soup, talking about a radio, a route, a daughter, a house. The work did not make them fall in love. The staying after it did. There is a difference, and they know it, which keeps the love from becoming a story they tell about their own goodness. It is a story about a sponge, passed across a sink, and a Saturday that did not end when the line did.

**End.**
