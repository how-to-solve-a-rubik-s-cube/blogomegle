The sign said the gate closed at dusk, and the sky was already practicing. Peter was still at the rail, photographing a city that did not need his help to be beautiful. He was thirty-six. Thandi came up the path with the key and the voice she used for visitors who were not trying to be a problem and were about to be one by accident.

"You have eight minutes," she said. "I am Thandi. I would like to lock this gate and also like you not to twist an ankle proving a point. The light goes faster than the brochure."

"Peter. I will come now. Eight minutes is generous. I lost a camera to a tide once. I can learn."

He came. The path down was steep and, in the blue minutes, honest about its stones. Thandi walked ahead and named the bad steps without turning it into a tour. Peter put the camera away, which she noticed as a form of respect. At the gate he waited while she locked it, and the click was satisfying, a day closed properly.

"I am parked at the lower lot," he said. "If your truck is the other way, I will not invent a reason to follow you. If it is the same way, we are both walking the road, and I would rather walk it as two people than as a man trailing a ranger. Thirty-six. I make films about buildings, not about women locking gates. You are safe from my job."

"Forty. The truck is the same way. Walk where I can see you. The road has drivers who think dusk is a suggestion."

They walked on the gravel shoulder. Thandi told him she had grown up on the flats and taken the ranger job because the mountain was a public thing and she intended to keep it that way. Peter told her he was in the city for a month, filming a library addition, and that he was renting a room with a bad kettle. She recommended a kettle. He wrote it down, an act so plain she smiled.

The month included a dinner that was not on the mountain, at a place she chose, where nobody asked her for a recommendation about a hike. Peter liked her in a chair as much as on a path. When the filming ended he took another contract he had already been offered, and he told her the offer was real so she would not think the city was a performance. She checked, once, with a friend who knew the production company. The offer was real. She told him she had checked. He said he would have done the same.

They still walk down before the gate. Sometimes it is her shift and he meets her at the lot with the better kettle's cousin, a thermos. Sometimes they are both off the clock and the mountain is only a shape. The last safe path is a habit and also a preference. Neither of them is interested in the version of love that stays up on the rail too long and calls the risk a feeling. They lock the gate. They walk where the other can see. The city does the shining without them.

**End.**
