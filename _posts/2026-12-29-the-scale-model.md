The model was cardboard and tape, the size of a real chair, and it did not fit through the idea of Anita's doorway. Greg stood in her kitchen with the tape measure and the humility of a man whose beautiful drawing had met a radiator. He was thirty-seven. She was thirty-five and had hired him to replace a chair that had died under an uncle, a death the uncle had survived and the chair had not.

"If I shrink the arms, it will look like a school chair," Greg said. "If I keep the arms, you will have a sculpture you cannot use. I am not interested in sculptures. I am interested in you sitting down."

"Then shrink the arms," Anita said. "I teach all day. I do not need a chair that performs. I need one that clears the radiator and holds a person who grades papers at midnight. Make it that. Make it pretty after."

He made it that. The second model, thinner at the elbows, slid past the radiator with a centimeter to spare. Anita sat in the cardboard, which collapsed slightly and made them both laugh, and declared the height correct. Greg wrote the number on the tape itself. They had coffee because the project had reached the point where not having coffee would have been rude, and the coffee became a conversation that was not about chairs: a school she loved, a workshop he shared with two other makers, a city that was cold already and not sorry.

"I will build it in the next two weeks," Greg said. "If you want updates, I will send them. If you want to come see the frame before the seat goes on, come. I am better in person than in a photograph. Thirty-seven. The chair is the job. The visit is not a charge."

Anita went. The workshop smelled like glue and winter coats. The frame was honest. She ran a hand along a joint and did not pretend to know more than she knew. Greg explained one thing and stopped. That restraint was what she thought about on the drive home, more than the wood.

The finished chair cleared the radiator. It holds her and, on Thursdays, him, because the coffee became a standing night and the standing night became a key. He did not build her a new life. He built a thing that fit the life she had, and then he asked, separately, whether there was room for him in it. There was. The centimeter of clearance remains. Anita likes it. It is proof that a beautiful idea can change its arms and still be wanted, and that a person can too.

**End.**
