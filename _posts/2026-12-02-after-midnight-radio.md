Willa was twenty-six and produced the overnight show, which meant she answered the phone, kept the log, and refused to become anyone's granddaughter on the air. Harold called at twelve-forty. He was sixty-eight, a retired engineer, and he wanted a song his wife had liked. He said wife in the past tense and did not linger on it.

"Play it if you can," he said. "I am Harold. I do not need a story around it. The song knew her better than a speech will."

Willa put it in the queue and told the host to read the title only. In the studio kitchen she ate yogurt and thought, not for the first time, that the night shift was a parish. She went home at two. The show went on without her.

Margaret heard the song in a kitchen of her own, sixty-four, washing a single plate. She had loved the same record in a different marriage, also ended, also not a story she wanted a host to improve. She wrote to the station on paper, because email felt flimsy for this, and addressed it to the caller named Harold, care of the show. The letter was short. It said: I am Margaret. I am sixty-four. I heard your song. My husband liked the B side. If you ever want coffee with a person who will not make you narrate your grief, I am at the bakery on Ingersoll on Tuesdays at eight.

Willa received the letter, read it because that was the job, and forwarded it to the number Harold had left. She did not add advice. She was twenty-six and competent and not the point.

Harold went on Tuesday with the letter in his pocket and a skepticism he considered healthy. Margaret was at a corner table with a book she was not reading. They recognized each other by the simple method of saying the names.

"I did not call for a response," Harold said.

"I know," Margaret said. "I responded anyway. You may sit. You may also take a cinnamon roll to go and leave. I have survived both outcomes in other years."

He sat. The roll was shared because it was large and because breaking a thing in half is a useful rehearsal. Harold talked about bridges he had inspected and a workshop he still kept, not about the chair in his house that he did not sit in. Margaret talked about a library job and a daughter in Ames. The dead were named once, clearly, and then the coffee was allowed to be coffee.

They met the next Tuesday and the one after. Willa saw Harold's number on the log less often, which she took as a good sign and did not investigate. Once, months later, he called to request the B side and asked the host to say it was for Margaret, who prefers the other side of things. Willa, back on a fill-in shift, smiled and let the line go through. She went home to her own life, which was not theirs.

Harold and Margaret are a Tuesday. They are also, now, a workshop where she reads while he sands a board, and a kitchen where two plates get washed. The song is still good. The producer is still young and correctly peripheral. The love story belongs to the two people who wrote and answered, and to the mornings they chose after the radio had done its small, sufficient job.

**End.**
