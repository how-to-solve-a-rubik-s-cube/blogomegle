The outlet was under a seat that did not want to admit it. Mei had found it first and was sitting on the floor with her back against a pillar, which in an airport is a kind of citizenship. She was twenty-nine. Hassan arrived with a phone at four percent and the expression of a man doing math he disliked. He was forty-four.

"There is room for a second cable," Mei said. "I am not guarding a socket. I am guarding my sanity. Sit if you can fold."

"I can fold," Hassan said. "Thank you. I am Hassan. My flight is in two hours and my book is on the phone, which was a poor architectural choice."

"Mei. Mine is in ninety minutes. I have an actual book. I will not gloat."

She did not gloat. She offered the paperback after her own chapter, and he took it as a loan, not a personality test. The charger brick warmed under their hands. Around them the terminal performed its announcements. Hassan was on his way back to a clinic where he did intake, not medicine, a distinction he made so she would not tell him a symptom. Mei was a lighting designer between a job in one mall and a job in another, and she talked about glare the way other people talk about weather. He liked the specificity. She liked that he did not compete with it.

When her phone reached a survivable number she unplugged and stayed, because the conversation had not reached a survivable end.

"I live here," Mei said. "If your layover is only a layover, we can stop at a pleasant anecdote. If you come through next month, as you said you might, I eat at a hawker center near my office on Wednesdays. I will be the person arguing with a lamp in a photograph. Twenty-nine, so the anecdote has a setting."

"Forty-four. I will come through. I will not make you responsible for recognizing me in a crowd. I will find the lamp."

He found the lamp in November, and the woman arguing with it, and he waited until she won. They ate at the center, standing at first, then sitting when a table opened as if the evening had decided to be kind. Hassan was not a layover anymore. His clinic was hiring a coordinator and he had taken the interview seriously, which Mei respected more than a grand move made for a stranger. He got the job for ordinary reasons. He told her the reasons. She celebrated with a second plate and no speech about destiny.

The outlet is still under that seat for other stranded phones. Mei and Hassan have better lighting at home, and a charger that reaches both sides of the sofa. When the power fails, which it rarely does, they tell the airport story without exaggerating the battery. Four percent was enough to ask for a Wednesday. The rest was showing up when the lamp, and the life, were actually there.

**End.**
