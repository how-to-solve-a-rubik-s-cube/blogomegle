The scarf had spent the winter on the back of a chair that was not Erik's. Astrid had meant to post it and had not, because a parcel felt like a scene and a scarf was only wool. In April she walked it to his building. She was twenty-eight. He was twenty-nine and happened to be on the step, killing time before a train, which would have been convenient in a film and was only inconvenient in life, because neither of them had prepared a face.

"Yours," she said. "Washed. I used my soap by accident the first time and then I rewashed it. It may still be confused."

"I can survive confused wool," Erik said. "Thank you. You could have kept it. Winter is long."

"I did not want a relic," Astrid said. "We ended kindly and then we acted like kindness was a wall. I would like the wall to have a gate. Not a large gate. Coffee, if you have a later train."

He had forty minutes. They used thirty, so he would not have to run. The cafe was new to both of them, which helped. Nothing on the menu had been theirs. Erik worked at a harbor office now, a job he described without glamour. Astrid was finishing an architecture license and lived with two friends and a fern. They had broken up over a move she had needed to make for the license and he had taken as a verdict on the city and on him. Both interpretations had been partly wrong. The coffee did not fix the year. It put the year on the table, where it could be seen without being worn.

"I was proud," Erik said. "Pride is not the same as a reason."

"I was busy," Astrid said. "Busy is not the same as gone. I am here with a scarf. That is the size of today."

The size was right. He caught his train. She went to a studio. On Saturday he asked, by text, whether the fern and the friends could spare her for a walk that did not end at a station. They walked. The scarf stayed in his drawer, clean, no longer a messenger. What they resumed was not the old arrangement. Astrid's license was still the plan. Erik's harbor job could be done in more than one city, a fact he investigated before he offered it, so the offer would be a schedule and not a sacrifice thrown at her feet.

They live, now, in a flat with better light for drawing. The scarf is just a scarf. Sometimes she wears it. The soap is theirs. The grand gesture never arrived, which is why the small one worked: a washed thing, a later train, a person who did not keep a relic when she could have a conversation instead.

**End.**
