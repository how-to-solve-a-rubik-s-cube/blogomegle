The corner table was for one, which meant a small lamp and a view of other people's conversations. Freja took it on a Thursday because her flat was full of drying paint and her head was full of a brief she had already finished. She was thirty-eight. The waiter did not make a speech about dining alone. He brought the fish and left her with it.

On Friday the table was free again, and so was Magnus, who had eaten there on Wednesday and remembered the lamp. He was forty-one. He sat. Halfway through his meal he recognized the woman from the night before, not as a mystery, as a person having the same idea. She recognized him by the coat on the chair.

"We are repeating ourselves," Freja said. "I am Freja. I am not lonely in the way the table implies. I am avoiding fumes."

"Magnus. I am avoiding a kitchen I did not clean. We can nod and go back to our books, or we can admit the coincidence is mildly funny. I vote for funny. It improves the fish."

They voted for funny. The waiter, on the second pass, brought bread without being asked and did not ask whether they were together. By the end of the plate they were, at least for the length of a conversation about paint colors and a kitchen. Freja designed wayfinding for hospitals. Magnus taught biology at a night school and liked the students who had already lived a shift before class. Neither of them was collecting a mood. They were comparing practicalities. It was more intimate than a mood.

"Tomorrow the paint will be dry," Freja said. "If you want a third night, come anyway. If two is the correct number for a coincidence, we can stop while we are ahead."

"I would like the third night to be a plan," Magnus said. "Plans are less mystical. I am free. Forty-one, since the lamp flatters everyone equally."

"Thirty-eight. The lamp is a liar and I accept the plan."

The third night the waiter set two glasses when he saw them at the door. They laughed, caught, and ordered. A month of Fridays followed, then a key, then a disagreement about a rug that they settled by putting the rug in the hall where both of them could trip on it and agree it was wrong. The corner table still gets them once a month, for the fish and for the pleasure of being known by a room. The paint is long dry. The kitchen is clean more often, because two people see it. What changed was not that a table for one became a table for two. It was that both of them could have kept eating alone, capably, and chose the chair opposite instead.

**End.**
