The plant did not belong to either of them, which was why it lived.

Helen was forty-five and had put the fern on the fire escape because her radiator made the inside of the apartment feel like a complaint. Mark was forty-eight, two floors up, and had started watering it on the nights she worked late at the hospital. He did not tell her. The fern told her, by not dying.

They met on a Thursday when both of them reached for the watering can he had left on her landing. Boston was doing its March trick of being cold and bright at once. Helen had a badge still clipped to her coat. Mark had a grocery bag and the look of a man who had rehearsed a sentence and then disliked it.

"I have been watering your plant," he said. "I am Mark. Forty-eight. I live up there. I am not building up to a request."

"Helen. Forty-five. It is not my plant. It came with a roommate who moved to Providence and called the fern a joint custody situation, which I ignored."

"Then I have been watering a legal ambiguity," Mark said.

"You have been watering a fern. Thank you."

They stood with the can between them. A train went by under the hill and made the grate buzz. Helen learned he taught night classes in building codes, which sounded like a punishment until he described a student who had finally understood a stair width and looked relieved, as if the city had become slightly less likely to fail her. Mark learned Helen worked in records, not in an operating room, and was tired of people telling her that records were not the real hospital. Records were how the real hospital remembered what it had done.

The fern stayed on the landing. They made a schedule on the back of a takeout menu. Helen watered on the mornings after a shift. Mark watered on the evenings she circled. If both of them watered, the fern forgave them. Ferns are less fragile than people pretend.

In May the schedule became coffee on the steps, because the menu had run out of blank space and neither of them wanted a group chat for a plant. Helen brought the good mugs and accepted the risk. Mark brought a piece of ginger cake from a bakery that closed too early and was therefore always a plan. They talked about a leak in his ceiling and a brother she had not called. He did not offer to fix her life. She did not offer to file his. They fixed the small things that had handles.

By October the fern had been moved inside Helen's window, because the first real cold was a fact and not a mood. Mark still came down with the cake. He knocked. She said come in, and meant the room, not a speech. He wiped his shoes. The radiator knocked. The plant went on being alive, which was the whole of its talent, and they went on being two adults who had decided that a shared chore could become a shared evening without anyone having to lose a name.

They still keep the schedule. It is on the fridge now, in handwriting they can both read. The happy part is not that the fern survived them. It is that they stopped pretending they were only meeting because of it.
