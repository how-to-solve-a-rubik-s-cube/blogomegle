The drawer stuck in damp weather, which meant the ring was sometimes literally hard to reach, a joke neither of them forced. Megan had put it there after a proposal that was not a proposal, a year ago, when Scott had said he wanted to marry her and she had said she wanted to want that, and they had agreed not to make the want into a scene. She was thirty-nine. He was forty-one. The ring was a simple band. It had not been thrown. It had been given a place.

On a Sunday the drawer opened easily. Megan took the box out and set it on the kitchen table between the eggs and the mail.

"I am not staging a moment," she said. "I am saying the year is up and the drawer is not a plan. I love you. I still do not want a wedding with a timeline designed by other people. I do want a life with you that has a name. Help me pick the name. Do not pick it for me."

Scott moved the mail so the box could be a box and not a centerpiece. "The name can be marriage and the wedding can be a Tuesday," he said. "Or the name can be something we write ourselves and the ring can go back in the drawer for good. I want you. I wanted you before the ring. The ring was my attempt to be clear and it landed as pressure. I am sorry for the landing. I am not sorry for the clarity."

They ate the eggs. They let the box stay out all morning, which turned out to be the trick: a symbol that had to survive ordinary hours. Megan went to a hardware store and came back. Scott answered an email. The box did not glow. It sat. At noon she put the band on for ten minutes, to feel the weight, and took it off again, and he did not flinch at the off. That was when she decided.

"Tuesday," she said. "The clerk, two friends, lunch after. The ring comes out of the drawer that morning and does not go back. If we want a party later, it will be a party, not a proof."

They did the Tuesday. The two friends cried in a controlled way and then were hungry, which kept the day human. The ring is on her hand now. The drawer still sticks in August and holds, instead, a stack of warranties and a photograph of the lunch. Scott and Megan tell the story as a year of waiting that was not indecision. It was respect. A drawer is a fine place for a question. It is a bad place to leave an answer that has already been found.
