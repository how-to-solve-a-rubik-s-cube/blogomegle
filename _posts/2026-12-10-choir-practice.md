There were eleven altos and ten folders. Ellen had arrived last because the bus had knelt for a wheelchair and she had not minded the wait. She was fifty-eight. Beverly, sixty-four, held the last folder and looked at the gap in the chairs.

"We can share," Beverly said. "I sing the right page. You turn it. If I rush, tap me. I am Beverly. I rush when I am nervous, and new people make me nervous in a way I am trying to treat as a hobby."

"Ellen. I turn pages for a living, or I used to, at a library desk. I can tap without making a moral of it."

They shared. Beverly rushed once, during a run of alleluias that had too many notes for the hour of the day. Ellen tapped her wrist, lightly, and Beverly came back to the beat with a face of such relief that Ellen had to look at the music to keep from staring. After practice the director thanked everyone in a blanket sentence. The altos put on coats. Ellen and Beverly were still holding the folder, neither quite releasing it.

"I walk down Grace Street," Beverly said. "If you walk any part of that, we could keep the folder as a pretext until the corner. Then I will give it back to the church like a good citizen."

"I walk most of that," Ellen said. "Keep the pretext."

The pretext lasted three blocks and then retired, because they were talking about a brother, a knee, a recipe for ham that had divided Beverly's family along theological lines. Ellen laughed so hard she had to stop walking. Beverly looked pleased in a private way. At Ellen's building they did the small dance of not ending the evening by accident.

"Thursday is not only the choir," Beverly said. "There is a diner that forgives early dinners. I am asking you to the diner, not to a new personality. Sixty-four. I have the personality already."

"Fifty-eight. I will meet the personality at the diner. Five-thirty. I like a booth."

The booth became part of Thursday, and the folder became an object they could both hold without using it as an excuse. Ellen had been divorced for a decade and had built a life of precise, pleasant days. Beverly had never married and was done explaining that to congregations. They compared the explanations once, found them boring, and ordered pie. In the spring Ellen joined the choir on purpose, not as a guest of a canceled plan. She stood beside Beverly. When the alleluias rushed, the tap was still available, a private metronome. They walk Grace Street after, folder or no folder. The church has bought a twelfth. They share one anyway, because some harmonies are logistical and some are chosen, and they like having both.

**End.**
