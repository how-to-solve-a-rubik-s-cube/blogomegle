The elevator was the size of a polite closet. It stopped between two and three with a sigh, as if the building were bored of helping. Beatriz pressed the alarm. A bell rang far away and then became someone else's problem. She was twenty-seven. Nuno, who had the only other square of floor, was fifty-one. He had a toolbox in his hand because the hotel had hired him to look at a window, not to be entombed with a guest.

"We will be out before it becomes a story," he said. "I am Nuno. If you need to sit, the floor is cleaner than it looks. I would not say that in a larger elevator."

"Beatriz. I will stand. I have a train in the morning and a sense of humor that lasts about ten minutes. You have used one."

They used the next four well. Not romantically, at first. Practically. He asked if she had water. She did, and she shared it. She asked if the hotel did this often. He said the elevator did it when the heat and the old motor agreed, and that the agreement was usually brief. He told her he lived in the city and fixed things that other people replaced. She told him she was a translator on a job that had ended, staying one extra night because the room was paid for and she was tired of airports. The age difference sat between them and was named, by her, before it could become a trick.

"I am twenty-seven," Beatriz said. "If we have a coffee after this, it will be because you were calm and specific, not because a box made us intimate. If that sentence is unwelcome, the door can open onto silence."

"The sentence is welcome," Nuno said. "I am fifty-one. I have a daughter older than you, which you should know before the coffee, not after. She is in Lisbon. She is not a secret. She is a fact."

The door opened onto a housekeeper who was more annoyed than alarmed. They were four minutes late to their own plans and otherwise fine. Beatriz still wanted the coffee. They had it in the lobby, where the light was ordinary and the toolbox was under the table, a reminder that he had a job and had finished the window after the elevator. Nuno did not linger into dinner. He said he would be at the same cafe at five the next day if her train could spare her. She changed the train to the later one and told him she had changed it, so the cost was visible.

The daughter met her on a weekend, not as a judge in a doorway, as a person at a table. It was not seamless. It was respectful, which Beatriz valued more. Nuno and Beatriz are still an odd pair on paper and a sensible pair in a kitchen. The elevator has been repaired. They do not ride it for nostalgia. Six minutes were enough to tell the truth about ages and daughters and a later train. Everything after that has been a choice made with the doors open.

**End.**
