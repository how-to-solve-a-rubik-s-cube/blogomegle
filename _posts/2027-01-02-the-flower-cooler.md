The cooler was a bright cold, all buckets and leaves. Fleur had been in since five because a wedding order did not care about her sleep. She was twenty-nine. Bram backed the van up at six with the day's stems and a face that apologized for existing so early. He was thirty-four. He had driven for the wholesaler for a year and had learned her name three weeks ago and used it carefully.

"Peonies are short," he said. "I brought an extra bucket of the white ones you do not hate. If that was wrong, they can go back. I would rather be wrong than leave you short on a Saturday."

"Leave them," Fleur said. "Wrong would be roses. You remembered. Come in before the van idles itself into a complaint. I have a thermos and one clean cup."

The cup was chipped and the coffee was strong. They drank in the narrow aisle between buckets, breath visible, wedding still ahead of her and route still ahead of him. Bram did not linger into her work. He asked one question about the order and then stopped. Fleur noticed the stop. People who love flowers often cannot stop talking. A man who can stop is a kind of rest.

"Same time tomorrow," she said. "Bring the short stems if you must and the cup will be clean. If you want a breakfast that is not a thermos after your route, I eat at nine near the market on days I am done. Today I am not done. Monday I am."

"Monday at nine," Bram said. "I will come as a person and not as a van. Thirty-four. The van is only my early costume."

Monday he was a person. They ate rolls and talked about a sister of his who thought flowers were a frivolous cargo, and a mother of hers who had taught her the names and then been surprised when the names became a shop. Bram had no speech about frivolity. He said people got married and people grieved and both of them needed something that would not last, delivered on time. Fleur looked at him over the roll and decided the sentence was better than flattery.

The thermos lives by the cooler door. Some mornings there are two cups. The wedding orders still ignore sleep. Bram still arrives at six and leaves when the buckets are in, unless she asks him to stay for a problem, in which case he stays and then goes, because the route is a promise to other shops. Fleur loves him for the leaving as much as for the extra white peonies. A life can be built at six in the morning, in the cold, out of someone remembering what you do not hate.

**End.**
