The loop took forty minutes if you did not stop for the view, and Grace always stopped for the view. She was sixty. The view was a harbor and a crane and a slice of mountain that appeared when the cloud had finished its shift. Martin stopped for the same slice. He was sixty-two. For three weeks they arrived at the bench within a minute of each other and treated this as a statistical curiosity.

"We are a timetable," Grace said on the day the curiosity became silly. "I am Grace. I walk at four because my clinic closes then. If I am crowding your bench, I can take the next one and still see the crane."

"Martin. You are not crowding it. I walk at four because if I wait until five I will talk myself out of the coat. Sit. I am a retired accountant. I can share a bench without invoicing you."

They sat. The crane lifted something that might have been hope or a container. Grace had spent her career fitting people for hearing aids and was used to watching faces for the moment a sound arrived. She watched Martin's face when a ferry horn blew. He heard it. Good. He noticed her noticing and raised an eyebrow, not offended.

"Occupational hazard," she said. "I cheer internally when a person gets the horn."

"Cheer externally if you want," Martin said. "I have been missing small sounds for years and pretending the world was getting ruder. It was me. The aids are new. The horn is a luxury."

The timetable held. They added a loop in the other direction so the walk would be longer than the sitting, a decision Martin proposed and Grace accepted because her knees liked a plan. He learned she had a daughter on the island and a freezer full of soup she had made during a lonely January and was still eating, which she found funny and he found reasonable. She learned he had divorced in his forties, survived it, and was not looking for a project. She was not applying to be one.

On a wet day they skipped the view and went to his apartment, which was high enough to see the crane anyway. Grace brought soup. Martin did the dishes as if the dishes were part of the conversation. The hearing aids sat on the table while he showered the rain off, and she did not touch them, a respect he clocked when he came back and saw them unmoved. Small things. They are the whole plot.

They still walk at four. Some days the mountain bothers to appear. Some days they are the view: two people in practical coats, sharing a bench they no longer pretend to have stumbled on, keeping a loop that is long on purpose so the hour will hold them both.

**End.**
