Amira meant to call her brother. The number she hit belonged to a man in a parked shuttle at the edge of the airport, who answered because the screen said a local area code and his shift had another hour of quiet. She was twenty-nine, still in scrubs, sitting on the curb outside a hospital in Phoenix because the break room had a television she could not bear. He was thirty-four.

"You are not Samir," she said.

"I am Luis," he said. "I drive a hotel van. If you need Samir I can hang up in a friendly way."

"Stay a minute," Amira said. "I pressed the wrong number with the right hand. I just want a voice that is not a monitor. I am a nurse. I am fine. No one in my care is the story. I am only tired."

Luis stayed. He did not ask for the kind of tired. He told her the van smelled like the last passenger's sunscreen, and that the heat had finally dropped enough for the steering wheel to be a normal object. She told him her brother was in Tucson and usually answered with a joke that was not as good as he thought. They laughed at a size that fit a curb and a parking lot.

"I am thirty-four," Luis said, because the call had become a conversation and he did not want her to build a younger man in her head. "I get off at midnight. I am not asking to come to a hospital curb. That is a bad plan."

"Twenty-nine," Amira said. "There is a diner on Van Buren that does not care what you are wearing. If you want to eat eggs at twelve-thirty and then go to your own home, I will be the person in the blue hoodie. If you do not, this was still a good wrong number."

He wanted the eggs. He came in the hoodie of his own, which made them briefly a club. The diner was bright and unromantic, and the waitress called everyone hon without investing in it. Amira ate as if she had forgotten food was allowed. Luis pushed the jam toward her and did not comment. They talked about a mother of his who still packed him fruit, and a neighbor of hers who played the same song every dawn. No one performed a rescue. The night had been hard in the ordinary professional way, and it was over.

They made the diner a Thursday. Sometimes she was on shift and left a message, the right number now, saved under his name and also under Van Buren so she would remember it was a place and not a spell. Luis learned her schedule without trying to optimize it. Amira learned he sang badly in the van and only when it was empty. The wrong number stayed in the call log for months, a small proof that a hand can miss and still arrive somewhere worth keeping. They did not mythologize the misdial. They showed up for eggs until the eggs became a life they both recognized in daylight.
