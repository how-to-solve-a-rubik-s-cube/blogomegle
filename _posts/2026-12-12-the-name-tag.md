The name tag said Karen in a handwriting that was not hers, because the registration desk had been in a hurry. She was fifty-four. The conference was about public libraries, a field she had entered after the life that included David. He was at the coffee urn, fifty-six, reading her tag with a care that made the urn overflow onto his saucer.

"You are going to scald yourself for a name," Karen said. "Hello. I am still Karen. I am not here to reenact anything."

"David. Good. Reenactments are for battlefields. This is a carpet. Your tag is crooked. May I say that, or is that already too much past?"

"You may say the tag is crooked. You may not say I look the same. I do not."

"You look like a person I am glad is in the room. That is the current sentence."

They went to different sessions. At lunch the only free seat was across from her, and he asked the table, not the past, whether it was free. It was. He sat. They talked about budgets and a branch that needed a roof. David was a facilities director for a school system. Karen was a deputy director now, a title she had earned without narrating it to men from her twenties. He did not ask her to narrate the years. She offered three facts: she had married, she had divorced, she had a son in college who was funny. He offered three: he had not married, he had raised a niece with his sister for a hard decade, the niece was thriving and bossy. They nodded at the facts like people accepting coats.

On the last evening the conference put them in a hallway again. Karen held her badge in her hand, done with it.

"I do not want a drink in a hotel bar," she said. "I want a walk to the lake and a conversation that is allowed to be ordinary. If you are still the sort of man who can be ordinary, come. Fifty-four. The age is so you do not do arithmetic out loud."

"Fifty-six. I can be ordinary. It is my best skill, acquired late."

The lake was a dark line. They walked. Nothing from the old apartment was required. Karen remembered a kitchen and did not open it. David remembered a fight about a move and did not plead his case. They had both been right to leave that version. This version had better posture. He called her the next week, after they were back in their own neighborhoods, and asked about the roof, and then about dinner. She said yes to the dinner and gave him the name of a place that did not have a past attached.

They are, carefully, a couple. The name tags are in a drawer, a joke they do not need to show anyone. What they perform now is a calendar: her board nights, his niece's Sundays, a lake when they can get to one. The old story stays unperformed. The new one is dull in all the ways that mean it might last. A crooked tag, straightened once, and then a life that does not need a badge to know who is in the room.

**End.**
