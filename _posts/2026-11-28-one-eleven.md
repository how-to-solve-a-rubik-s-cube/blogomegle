The request line opened at one. Brooke called at one-eleven because she had been awake anyway, icing a cake for a morning delivery, and the radio was the only other voice in the kitchen. She was twenty-seven. The song was an old one her father had played while fixing a sink, and she wanted it for no reason she was willing to make poetic.

Dale answered. He was forty-five, and his on-air voice was lower than his real one, a fact she would learn later and like. He took the request, asked the name, and did not ask her to tell the city a secret.

"Brooke," she said. "And if you play it, play it plain. No dedication. I am alone with a cake. That is not a tragedy. It is a job."

"Dale," he said, off the air, in the seconds before the song. "I will play it plain. For what it is worth, I am also alone with a job. It is a Tuesday in Omaha. We are not special. The song is still good."

He played it. She stood with a spatula and let the whole thing happen without filming it. At the end he back-announced the title and nothing else, which was exactly the contract. She called again after the song, a thing she had not planned.

"Thank you," Brooke said. "I am twenty-seven, since the hour makes people invent ages. The cake is for a hardware store that is opening. I will be asleep at a reasonable time tomorrow and I am not building a persona."

"Forty-five," Dale said. "I am done at three. There is a diner that serves breakfast to the night shift and does not care that breakfast is a theory. If you want eggs after the cake is finished, I will be the man with the headphones around his neck. If the cake runs long, the eggs will forgive you."

The cake did not run long. She went in flour on one sleeve and did not apologize for it. Dale had the headphones, as promised, and a face that was kinder than the voice and just as tired. They ate. He told her he had done the overnight for nine years and still liked the hour when the calls got honest. She told him she had learned icing from an aunt who believed butter was a moral good. He did not ask her to be a regular caller. She did not ask him to play their meeting on the air. Both omissions felt like manners.

Wednesdays became the diner, because that was the night his show ended and her orders did not start until noon. Sometimes they talked about songs. More often they talked about a leaking van, a brother, a landlord, the price of eggs. The request line stayed a request line. Once, months later, she called at one-eleven on purpose and asked for the sink song again, and this time she allowed a dedication, a small one: for the man who played it plain. He played it. He did not elaborate. After three he met her at the diner, and the headphones came off, and the rest of the night was theirs and not the city's. That was the shape they wanted. A public song. A private breakfast. A life that could tell the difference.

**End.**
