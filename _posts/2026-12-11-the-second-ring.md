Evelyn needed a washer the size of a freckle. The bin was a chaos of almosts. She was seventy-four and patient, which did not mean she wanted a stranger to solve her. Howard was already at the bin, seventy-one, holding a washer up to the light as if it might confess.

"If you are looking for three-eighths, they are hiding under the half-inch," he said. "I am only reporting. I am Howard. I will not take your aisle."

"Evelyn. Stay in the aisle. You have the light. I have the list. We can be a committee of two and then disband."

They found the freckle-sized one. Evelyn's faucet had dripped all night; she described it with the irritation of a person who lives alone and hears the house too clearly. Howard's storm door had a screw that would not bite. He described that. Neither of them said the word widow. It was in the way they talked about repairs that used to be someone else's job and were now theirs, without self-pity and without a medal.

At the register the cashier dealt with them as a pair, and they did not correct her, and then they stood outside correcting the record for themselves.

"I was married for forty-six years," Howard said. "She died in March of the year before last. I come here on Saturdays because the house is finished by noon and the day is not. If that is more than you wanted at a hardware store, you can leave with the washer and a clean conscience."

"I wanted the truth more than a clean conscience," Evelyn said. "I was married for fifty-one. He died in his chair with the crossword half done. I hate that I remember the clue. I am free on Saturdays after the faucet. There is a lunch counter next door that makes a grilled cheese worthy of a committee."

They did not disband. The grilled cheese was worthy. Howard walked Evelyn home the long way, carrying nothing of hers, because she had declined the bag with a smile. On her porch she did not invite him in. She invited him to the counter next Saturday, which was a better invitation, being repeatable. He came. He kept coming. The faucet got its washer. The storm door got its screw. The larger repairs were slower: a holiday alone that was less alone, a meeting of children who were cautious and then kind, a ring each of them still owned and did not wear, put in a dish on a dresser, not thrown away.

They did not rush a second wedding. They took the aisle at the speed of people who have already done forever once and know it is made of Saturdays. Howard stays over when the ice is bad. Evelyn stays at his place when the crossword nostalgia is worse. The hardware store remains a useful place and not a shrine. They still help each other find the small exact thing, and they still call it a committee, because the joke is theirs, and because a life can start in a bin of washers without being small.

**End.**
