The play was a comedy about a kitchen, which meant a great many plates and a door that stuck on purpose. Beth played the sister. She was thirty-four and had auditioned because a friend dared her and because she missed being in a room that was not her clinic. Dennis played the neighbor. He was forty and had done the lights for years and had been promoted to a speaking part by a director who was desperate and correct.

They were bad in the first week. Beth rushed. Dennis forgot a line and replaced it with a truth that was not in the script, which made the stage manager laugh and then beg him to stop being interesting. After rehearsal they sat on the loading dock with the notes and did not go home at the speed of people escaping.

"I am better at the notes than at the line," Dennis said. "I am Dennis. If you want to run the kitchen scene once without an audience of folding chairs, my porch is quiet. If you want to forget the porch, we can limit this to being colleagues in a bad week."

"Beth. I want the porch. I am a nurse and I memorize worse when I am trying to be charming. Do not be a charm. Be a person with a script."

The porch was quiet. They ran the scene until the plates were mime and the lines were in their mouths. Then they sat and were people. Beth talked about a shift pattern and a brother. Dennis talked about the hardware store he managed and a decision, recent, to stop dating people who wanted him to be funnier than he was. Beth said the true line he had invented was funnier than the script. He looked so grateful she had to look at the actual street for a moment.

The play got better. Not good in a way that tours, good in a way that a town claps for. On closing night the door stuck for real and Dennis fixed it in character, which brought the house down and was not in the notes. Beth met him after with her own face on, greasepaint mostly gone.

"Same porch," she said. "No script. I want to know what you say when nobody has written it."

What he said was ordinary and exact. He liked her. He liked the clinic stories when she wanted to tell them and the silence when she did not. He would make dinner on Sundays if Sundays were free. Sundays were free. The play is over. The porch is a place they still sit, and the kitchen in the house they found together has a door that does not stick, because he fixed it offstage, for good. Beth still rushes when she is nervous. Dennis still tells the truth when he forgets. Between them, those are not flaws. They are cues.

**End.**
